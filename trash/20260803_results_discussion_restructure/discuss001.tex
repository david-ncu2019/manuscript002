\subsection{Monthly compaction assessment during delayed data delivery}
\label{subsec:discussion_delay}

The Tuku experiment examined a narrow but practical monitoring problem. Monthly MLCW measurements were assumed to continue, but their release was delayed for six months. During that interval, contemporaneous hydraulic head and cGNSS observations provided provisional estimates of compaction in six depth sections. The principal result was \placeholder{INTERPRET THE UPDATED MODEL RELATIVE TO THE BASELINES}. Direct MLCW observations nevertheless remained essential because they provided the reference measurements needed to evaluate and periodically update the model.

The analysis estimated conditions during the target month rather than forecasting future groundwater behavior. Each estimate used hydraulic head and cGNSS records observed in that same month. The resulting capability is therefore best understood as temporary completion of a delayed monitoring record. It should not be described as a six-month forecast or as a replacement for subsurface compaction measurements.

Previous data-driven studies reconstructed missing compaction observations from groundwater, rainfall, groundwater-use, and land-use information \citep{liu_reconstructing_2023, liu_deep_2025}. The present study extended this line of work by estimating monthly increments within six standardized depth sections and withholding consecutive observations in temporal order. \placeholder{COMPARE FINAL PERFORMANCE WITH PRIOR STUDIES USING COMPATIBLE METRICS ONLY}. The ordered evaluation more closely represented delayed data delivery than a random division of the historical record because every estimate depended only on information available at that time.

\subsection{Implications of less frequent MLCW measurements}
\label{subsec:discussion_reduced_sampling}

Delayed data delivery and reduced measurement frequency create different information gaps. The evaluated design assumed that six monthly MLCW observations existed and became available together at the end of each block. A field schedule with one MLCW measurement every six or twelve months would instead provide only the total compaction accumulated between two visits. That endpoint difference would not reveal how compaction was distributed among the intervening months.

Monthly model estimates could still describe the likely timing of compaction between less frequent field visits, while the next MLCW measurement could test their accumulated total. However, the endpoint observation cannot be added to a monthly calibration dataset as six separate responses. A reduced-frequency application would require an update method that constrains the sum of the monthly estimates to the measured interval compaction. This requirement was tested directly by a no-update reduced-observation sensitivity analysis, described next.

The design of this analysis is described in \Cref{subsec:no_update_sensitivity}. At each of 22 rolling origins spaced six months apart, the Bayesian ridge regression model was fitted once using the same non-MLCW predictor set as the main analysis, with MLCW contributing only the pre-origin training responses. Twelve months of estimates were then produced with no further MLCW-derived input and no refit; the six-month result at each origin was the first six months of this same fit, not a separately fitted model. Across the 22 origins, the mean absolute endpoint error (\Cref{subsec:no_update_sensitivity}) was 4.96~mm at the six-month horizon and 8.18~mm at the twelve-month horizon (root mean square endpoint error 5.74~mm and 9.50~mm, respectively), and the mean signed endpoint error (bias) was 0.47~mm and 1.13~mm at the six- and twelve-month horizons, respectively. Both the mean absolute and mean signed endpoint error were therefore larger at the twelve-month horizon than at the six-month horizon.

The horizon-normalized error rate, equal to the mean absolute endpoint error divided by the number of months in the horizon, was 0.83~mm/month at six months and 0.68~mm/month at twelve months, a difference of $-0.1447$~mm/month (95\% CI $[-0.2945,-0.0173]$) by paired moving-block bootstrap. This negative difference should not be read as evidence that the twelve-month horizon produced more reliable estimates than the six-month horizon. Per-row monthly estimation error, computed before summing over a horizon, was approximately 0.35~mm/month at both six and twelve months, indicating that month-to-month estimation accuracy did not change with horizon length. The horizon-normalized rate can decrease with horizon length because it divides the absolute value of a signed cumulative sum by the number of months summed: within a longer horizon, positive- and negative-signed monthly errors can partially cancel before the absolute value is taken, lowering this ratio even while the mean absolute and mean signed endpoint error both increase with horizon length, as observed here. For this reason, the mean absolute and mean signed endpoint error reported above, rather than the horizon-normalized rate, are the statistics relevant to comparing the six- and twelve-month horizons; the horizon-normalized rate is reported only for comparability across horizons of different length. The coefficient of determination was not computed for this analysis, and the reported confidence intervals reflect fewer than 22 independent windows because consecutive twelve-month horizons overlapped by six months.

\subsection{Limitations and practical scope}
\label{subsec:discussion_limitations}

The first limitation is the use of one monitoring site. The fitted relations reflect the Tuku sediment profile, well configuration, observation periods, and historical range of hydraulic conditions. Their performance cannot be transferred directly to another site. Evaluation at additional MLCW stations is required before the approach can support broader application across the Choushui River Alluvial Fan.

The second limitation concerns the information contained in the predictors. Surface displacement combines deformation above and below the deepest MLCW ring, and groundwater wells observe hydraulic head only within their screened intervals. The local lithological log adds depth information but does not describe time-varying material properties. In addition, the prediction intervals depend on a limited archive of earlier errors and are unavailable at the beginning of the evaluation. These constraints should be considered when the estimates are used to identify short periods of elevated compaction.

Within these limits, the study provides a transparent local test of monitoring continuity. The model does not remove the need for MLCW observations. Instead, it uses more frequently available hydraulic and surface displacement records to bridge a known delay and then incorporates the direct compaction measurements when they arrive. This division of roles preserves the MLCW as the reference observation while allowing monthly subsurface conditions to be assessed before the monitoring record is complete.
