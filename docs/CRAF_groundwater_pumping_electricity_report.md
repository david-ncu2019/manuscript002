## 1. Introduction

The Choushui River Alluvial Fan (CRAF; Chinese: 濁水溪沖積扇) in central-western Taiwan is one of the most agriculturally productive and hydrogeologically stressed regions in East Asia. Covering approximately 1,800 km² across Changhua and Yunlin counties, the fan supports intensive rice, sugarcane, and mixed-crop cultivation that depends heavily on groundwater extraction. The interplay between seasonal agricultural demand, sustained over-pumping since the mid-1970s, and the compressible fine-grained sediments of the distal and mid-fan has produced some of the most severe land subsidence rates observed in the world — exceeding 14 cm/year in localized areas [10] and cumulative displacements of more than 210 cm by 2009 [1].

Three interlocking problems define the hydrogeological challenge of the CRAF:

1. **Groundwater level (GWL) fluctuations**: Seasonal rainfall and irrigation patterns drive large annual oscillations in piezometric head, superimposed on a long-term declining trend caused by cumulative over-extraction.
2. **Pumping volumes**: Because private groundwater wells in Taiwan are neither metered nor comprehensively licensed, actual extraction volumes are largely unknown. They must be estimated from irrigation records, groundwater hydrograph analysis, or proxy data such as electricity consumption.
3. **Land subsidence**: GWL decline compresses compressible clay-rich aquitards irreversibly (inelastic compaction), producing permanent ground settlement that threatens the High-Speed Rail (HSR), coastal flood defences, and infrastructure throughout the fan.

This report synthesises the current state of knowledge on all three topics, drawing on peer-reviewed literature spanning 1992–2024, with the aim of providing an integrated, quantitative description of the CRAF groundwater system that can inform both scientific research and water-resource management.

## 2. Study Area and Hydrogeological Setting

### 2.1 Geography and Geology

The CRAF is formed by alluvial deposits of the Choushui River (濁水溪), which flows from east (Central Mountain Range) to west (Taiwan Strait). The fan extends from the south bank of the Wu River in the north to the Puzi River in the south, and from the Bagua Mountain Platform and Douliu Hills in the east to the Taiwan Strait coast in the west [3, 4].

Geologically, sediment grain size decreases systematically from the proximal fan (gravel-dominated) through the mid-fan (interbedded sand and clay) to the distal fan (fine sand, silt, and compressible clay). This transition governs both the aquifer architecture and the susceptibility to land subsidence.

### 2.2 Aquifer Architecture

The central and coastal portions of the CRAF, within 330 m depth, contain four principal aquifers separated by three aquitards [3, 4]:

| Aquifer | Approximate depth (m) | Primary lithology |
|---------|----------------------|-------------------|
| Aquifer I (F1) | 0–50 | Sand with gravel |
| Aquifer II (F2) | 50–150 | Fine-to-medium sand |
| Aquifer III (F3) | 150–250 | Fine sand and clay interbeds |
| Aquifer IV (F4) | > 250 | Deep sand/gravel |

Aquifer II has historically been the most heavily exploited because of its large spatial extent and accessible depth for both agricultural and domestic wells [5]. Aquitards T1–T3, which separate these units, consist of compressible silts and clays that are the primary locus of irreversible inelastic subsidence.

At the **proximal fan** (near the mountain front), the four aquifers merge into a continuous gravel body with no significant confining layers, allowing unrestricted groundwater recharge and flow. Moving toward the **distal fan**, aquitards thicken and confine the underlying aquifers, so that piezometric heads in the confined units reflect the integrated effect of recharge from the eastern fan apex minus regional extraction [3].

### 2.3 Monitoring Networks

The Taiwan Water Resources Agency (WRA) and the Central Geological Survey (CGS) jointly established the Groundwater Monitoring Network Plan (1992–2009), which installed 223 monitoring wells in four aquifer layers across the CRAF [3]:

- Aquifer I: 73 wells
- Aquifer II: 87 wells
- Aquifer III: 47 wells
- Aquifer IV: 17 wells

A complementary network of 50 continuous GNSS stations (CRAF GNSS_NET) has recorded surface displacement since 2011 [3]. Multi-Layer Compaction Monitoring Wells (MLCWs; 磁環式分層壓縮計) at 37–39 sites provide the only direct measurement of per-depth compaction at 5 m intervals from 0 to 300 m below ground surface [4].

## 3. Seasonal and Long-Term Groundwater Level Fluctuations

### 3.1 Seasonal Patterns

GWL in the CRAF exhibits pronounced seasonal oscillations driven by the monsoon climate and irrigation calendar. The wet season (approximately May–October) brings the majority of annual rainfall (~1,200–2,000 mm/year), recharging the proximal fan aquifers and transmitting pressure head downgradient to confined aquifers within the mid-fan. The dry season (November–April) sees reduced recharge and continued agricultural extraction, causing GWL to decline.

Annual GWL oscillation amplitudes generally **increase from northeast to southwest** across the CRAF [3], reflecting (1) greater confinement and storage depletion in the distal fan, and (2) higher extraction intensity in the agricultural coastal zone. Time-series analysis of GNSS and groundwater monitoring data from 2011 to 2022 found that the amplitude of annual GWL changes and the amplitude of annual vertical GNSS displacement are spatially correlated and both peak in the Yunlin coastal zone [3].

The interaction between seasonal GWL change and ground-surface elevation depends critically on the local hydrogeological regime:

- **Proximal fan (unconfined aquifer, Model M13)**: GWL and surface elevation are **negatively correlated** — rising water table in the wet season increases the load on deeper, non-recharged layers, compressing them and causing the surface to sink slightly; falling water table in the dry season reduces the load and the surface rises. The elastic response at the Pingding (坪頂) site is approximately 0.034 cm of surface uplift per 1-metre fall in unconfined water-table elevation [1].

- **Distal fan (confined aquifer, Model M23)**: GWL and surface elevation are **positively correlated** — rising piezometric head in the confined aquifer reduces effective stress in the clay-rich aquitards and allows elastic rebound; falling head increases effective stress and causes compaction. At the Hefeng (和豐) coastal site, each 1-metre decline in confined aquifer head causes 0.176 cm of surface settlement [1].

### 3.2 Long-Term Depletion Since the 1970s

A sustained increase in groundwater extraction beginning approximately in 1976 (民國65年) initiated a long-term decline in piezometric heads across all four aquifers. Historical annual-average groundwater levels at four representative stations, measured in metres above mean sea level (MSL), are summarised below [1]:

| Station | Pre-1976 average (m MSL) | Current average (m MSL) | Total decline (m) |
|---------|-------------------------|------------------------|-------------------|
| Ershui (二水) | ~80 | ~40 | ~40 |
| Jiaxing (嘉興) | ~25 | ~5 | ~20 |
| Beigang (北港) | ~8 | ~−14 | ~22 |
| Hefeng (和豐) | ~3 | ~−15 | ~18 |

Over the 35-year period 1976–2011, the total reduction in groundwater storage across the CRAF system was approximately **2.5 billion m³**, with an average annual deficit of about **70 million m³/year** [1].

The most severe hotspot identified in recent studies [3] records a GWL decline rate of **0.54 m/year** in the worst-affected Yunlin zone, accompanied by GNSS-measured subsidence of 4.2–5.2 cm/year.

## 4. Groundwater Pumping: Volumes and Sources

### 4.1 Categories of Water Use

Because the CRAF includes multiple crop types with staggered irrigation seasons, pumping demand is temporally and spatially heterogeneous. Pumping can be classified into:

1. **Non-irrigation pumping** (domestic, industrial, aquaculture, livestock): relatively stable throughout the year; estimated annual rate treated as constant.
2. **Irrigation pumping**: highly seasonal; dominated by two rice-growing seasons (Period I: January–May; Period II: June–October) plus year-round miscellaneous crops [6].

Within irrigation, water-use intensity varies by crop type: paddy rice (稻作) has the highest unit-area demand, while vegetables, sugarcane, corn, sweet potato, and peanuts have lower but non-negligible demand. The simultaneous cultivation of multiple crop types across different sub-irrigation districts means that total monthly pumping cannot be characterised by a single irrigation type.

The well inventory for Yunlin County alone, compiled by Hung et al. [11], totals **125,699 pumping wells**: 115,397 agricultural (shallow, 0–100 m depth, targeting F1/F2), 356 industrial (deep, targeting F3/F4), 4,398 domestic, 5,102 aquaculture, and 446 other. Tatas and Chu [12] independently report >100,000 pumping wells distributed across Yunlin County. The dominant share of agricultural wells at shallow depths means that most extraction targets the first and second aquifers, while the small number of deep industrial wells disproportionately affects F3 and F4.

### 4.2 Estimation Methodologies

Because private well pumping is not systematically metered, pumping volumes must be estimated. Three complementary methods have been applied in the CRAF:

**Method 1: Groundwater hydrograph analysis (水位歷線法)**  
Hsu et al. [6] developed a procedure specifically designed for the CRAF's multiple-irrigation-practice regime. The slope of the groundwater storage hydrograph during consecutive non-rainy days in the non-irrigation dry season gives the stable non-irrigation pumping rate. During irrigation periods, the slope changes to reflect both non-irrigation pumping and seasonal irrigation pumping; combining this slope with records from the Irrigation Association (農田水利會) allows seasonal irrigation pumping rates to be estimated. Monthly pumping volumes are the product of pumping rate × pumping days.

**Method 2: Spatial regression from drawdown**  
Chu et al. [5] developed a spatially varying regression model that estimates annual subsidence from accumulated drawdown in each aquifer layer. Fitting the model to leveling and monitoring-well data from 2015 simultaneously estimates the spatial pattern of the skeletal storage coefficient *S_k* and identifies the spatial relationship between pumping-induced drawdown and resulting subsidence, without requiring a full numerical groundwater flow model. The model achieved RMSE ≤ 0.8 cm for annual subsidence across 662 leveling points.

Total groundwater extraction in the CRAF has been estimated at approximately **240,000 m³/day** (~87 million m³/year), with the majority drawn from Aquifers I and II [5]. Changhua County's agricultural sector uses approximately 1.8 billion m³/year and Yunlin's approximately 1.5 billion m³/year for irrigation [5].

## 5. Electricity Power Usage as a Proxy for Pumping Volume Estimation

### 5.1 Rationale

In Taiwan, groundwater extraction from private agricultural and domestic wells is not directly metered. However, electric pumps are the dominant technology for groundwater withdrawal in the CRAF, and their electricity consumption is billed and recorded by the utility. This creates an indirect monitoring pathway: **electricity consumed by pumping motors can be converted to pumped volume** using pump motor efficiency and head-discharge relationships.

Tatasac et al. [2] exploited this relationship to produce the first regional-scale map of annual pumping volumes for the entire CRAF, subsequently linking those volumes to observed land subsidence rates through time-dependent spatial regression.

### 5.2 Key Findings (Tatasac et al., 2022/2023)

The study region was the Choushui River Alluvial Fan. The main methodological and empirical findings are:

1. **Pumping-volume estimation**: Electricity consumption data from Taipower (台電) were combined with pump specifications and local head conditions to estimate spatially distributed pumping volumes at the sub-district level for multiple years.

2. **Spatial heterogeneity of the subsidence–pumping relationship**: Pumping volumes and subsidence hotspots are **not spatially coincident**. The maximum water-level cone of depression occurs in the distal fan, but the calculated subsidence bowl is located further inland. This spatial lag reflects the heterogeneous compressibility of geological materials: inelastic storage coefficients peak in inland mid-fan clay-rich layers, not in the pumping centres themselves [2, 5].

3. **Time-dependent spatial regression model**: A time-dependent spatial regression approach improved model performance and reduced spatial data heterogeneity relative to ordinary least squares. The average RMSE of annual subsidence across the CRAF was **0.65 cm**, indicating a high level of correlation between electricity-inferred pumping and observed subsidence.

4. **Management simulation**: The model was used to simulate the effect of **reducing pumping withdrawals** on subsidence rates. This predictive capability is directly relevant to best-management-practice design for the CRAF [2].

5. **Practical limitation**: Spatial regression provides reliable estimates at the sub-district level but does not resolve individual well contributions. It is best applied to annual time steps and areas with relatively uniform pump technology [2].

### 5.3 Cross-Validation with Irrigation Records

An earlier NTU thesis (Huang, 2017) developed a K-means clustering and artificial neural network (ANN) approach to relate electricity consumption patterns to pumping volumes, using irrigation association records as ground truth. The method demonstrated that monthly electricity billing data, when combined with local pump efficiency curves and cluster analysis to distinguish crop types, could estimate pumping volumes with errors comparable to those of hydrograph-based methods. This establishes an independent line of evidence supporting the electricity-consumption proxy.

### 5.4 Seasonal Signature in Electricity Data

Because rice paddy irrigation accounts for the dominant share of agricultural electricity consumption, electricity usage exhibits a pronounced bimodal seasonal signal:

- **First rice season (Jan–May)**: Peak electricity consumption corresponding to planting and early growth.
- **Second rice season (Jun–Oct)**: Second peak, typically lower than the first.
- **Off-season (Nov–Jan)**: Reduced but non-zero consumption from domestic, industrial, and aquaculture use.

This temporal pattern aligns with the seasonal GWL declines observed in monitoring wells [1, 6], providing qualitative verification that electricity-estimated pumping captures the dominant seasonal signal.

## 6. Land Subsidence: Observations and Mechanisms

### 6.1 Observed Rates and Spatial Distribution

Land subsidence in the CRAF has been monitored continuously since the early 1990s using a multi-sensor network (precise leveling, GNSS, InSAR, MLCWs). Key observational facts from the literature are:

- **1992–2009**: Maximum cumulative subsidence of >210 cm in Dacheng Township, Changhua County; >130 cm in Yuanchang Township, Yunlin County [1].
- **2002**: Maximum annual rate 9.5 cm/year; exacerbated by drought [10].
- **2003**: Maximum annual rate 12.2 cm/year; the worst single-year record, coinciding with the most severe drought and highest pumping intensity.
- **1996–1999 (PSInSAR)**: Baojhou, Tuku, and Yuanchang Townships, Yunlin County — maximum line-of-sight subsidence ~7.8 cm/year; Lunbei Township ~3.5 cm/year [10].
- **2007**: After mitigation measures introduced from 2007 onward, the total area with annual subsidence > 3 cm/year decreased from 1,600 km² (2001) to 803 km² (2007) [10].
- **2011–2022 (GNSS)**: A new subsidence hotspot between Tuku and Yuanchang Townships in Yunlin shows rates of 4.2–5.2 cm/year [3, 4].
- **Below 300 m**: The most severe compaction occurs below 200–300 m depth, and compaction has been detected at depths > 300 m, suggesting deeper aquifer exploitation than previously recognised [4].
- **2017–2018 (precision leveling)**: Maximum annual rates exceeding 5 cm/year in Tuku and Yuanchang townships, confirming that the mid-fan hotspot persists [11].
- **2014–2024 (MLCW at TKJS supersite)**: Cumulative compaction of approximately **25 cm** over 10 years, with principal compression zones at 50–156 m and 241–295 m depth [13].
- **2021 drought**: During Taiwan's most severe drought in a century, groundwater levels dropped to record lows and extensometer data captured exceptional subsurface compaction rates [13].

### 6.2 Physical Mechanisms

**Elastic (recoverable) compaction**: When effective stress in an aquifer or aquitard fluctuates around values below the preconsolidation stress (the maximum historical effective stress), compaction is elastic and fully reversible upon head recovery. This is the dominant mode at the proximal fan and in shallow, sandy Aquifer I.

**Inelastic (permanent) compaction**: When declining piezometric head in a confined aquifer drives effective stress in the interbedded clay layers beyond the preconsolidation stress, pore water is expelled irreversibly from the clay matrix and compaction is permanent. At Hefeng (distal fan), once the confined head fell below approximately 0 m MSL (the pre-1975 historical minimum), each additional 1-metre fall below the previous minimum ultimately produces **7.34 cm of irreversible subsidence** [1]. The response rate is slow: from 1976 to 2009, the average clay-layer head fell to approximately −12.7 m MSL, producing ~93.2 cm of cumulative inelastic subsidence. Total projected subsidence from the current trajectory is ~132 cm once head reaches −18 m MSL [1].

### 6.3 Spatial Patterns and the Subsidence–Pumping Mismatch

One of the most important and counterintuitive findings in recent research [2, 5] is that **the location of maximum subsidence does not coincide with the location of maximum pumping**. The spatial regression analyses of Tatasac et al. [2] and Chu et al. [5] both find that:

- The largest water-level cone of depression occurs in the **distal fan** coastal zone where dense aquaculture and agricultural wells concentrate.
- The calculated subsidence bowl peaks **further inland** in the mid-fan, where compressible clay-rich aquitards are thickest and inelastic storage coefficients are largest.

This spatial offset has critical management implications: restricting pumping in the observed subsidence zone may be insufficient if the source aquifers supplying water to those zones are depressurised by pumping elsewhere.

Chu et al. [14] applied empirical orthogonal function (EOF) decomposition to monthly compaction data from 31 MLCW stations (January 2015 – December 2018) and resolved the offset into three quantifiable spatial modes:

- **EOF1 (97.5% of variance):** continuous long-term subsidence concentrated in the southern inland mid-fan. This is the dominant signature, driven by sustained agricultural pumping.
- **EOF2 (1.7%):** seasonal subsidence and rebound at the southern distal fan and proximal fan margins — elastic response to the monsoon wet–dry cycle.
- **EOF3 (0.4%):** high-frequency intra-seasonal fluctuations along the central coast, linked to aquaculture pumping for fish-pond water temperature control.

The timing of maximum subsidence follows a repeatable calendar: subsidence rates peak in **February–March** (late dry season), while the wet season runs June–September. The intra-annual calendar confirms that dry-season agricultural pumping is the primary driver of both the trend and the seasonal mode, while aquaculture adds a distinct higher-frequency signal in the coastal zone.

## 7. Interaction Models Between Groundwater Level and Ground Surface Elevation

The physical relationship between GWL change and surface elevation change depends on which hydraulic and mechanical process dominates. Two end-member models have been formalised for the CRAF [1]:

### 7.1 Model M13: Weight-Controlled (Unconfined Aquifer Dominant)

**Location**: Proximal fan, alluvial fan apex, and terraces (e.g., Pingding/坪頂 on the Douliu Hills).

**Dominant aquifer**: Unconfined (storage coefficient ~0.15).

**Physical mechanism**:
- Wet season: Rainfall recharges the unconfined aquifer. Water table rises, increasing the load (weight) on the underlying non-recharged confined layers. Despite the rise in pore water pressure reducing effective stress locally, the much larger weight increase compresses deeper, non-recharged layers more than the unconfined aquifer expands. **Net result: surface sinks.**
- Dry season: Water table falls, load decreases, surface rises.

**Observational signature**: GWL and surface elevation are negatively correlated (opposite direction of change). The Pingding elastic coefficient is 0.034 cm per metre GWL change [1].

### 7.2 Model M23: Pressure-Controlled (Confined Aquifer Dominant)

**Location**: Distal fan and mid-fan, near the coast (e.g., Hefeng/和豐 near the coast).

**Dominant aquifer**: Confined (storage coefficient ~0.0019).

**Physical mechanism**:
- Wet season: Lateral recharge from the proximal fan raises piezometric head in the confined aquifer. Rising pore pressure reduces effective stress in the clay aquitards, allowing elastic expansion. Because the storage coefficient is tiny, the added water mass (and its weight) is negligible. **Net result: surface rises.**
- Dry season: Head falls, pore pressure falls, effective stress rises, compaction, surface sinks.

**Observational signature**: GWL and surface elevation are positively correlated (same direction of change). The Hefeng elastic coefficient is 0.176 cm per metre GWL change [1]. When head falls below the preconsolidation level, the elastic response transitions to irreversible inelastic compaction with a coefficient of 7.34 cm/m.

### 7.3 Mixed Model M123

In transitional zones between proximal and distal fan, both unconfined loading and confined pressure effects operate simultaneously. The relative contribution of each determines the sign and magnitude of the GWL–elevation correlation. This complexity partly explains why spatial regression of pumping and subsidence requires spatially varying rather than uniform coefficients [2, 5].

### 7.4 Head threshold rule for unrecoverable subsidence

Tatas and Chu [12] extended the M13/M23 framework by developing a **statistical head threshold** that classifies subsidence as elastic (recoverable) or inelastic (unrecoverable) using only hydraulic head data. The threshold is defined as:

```
h* = AVE − α · SD
```

where AVE and SD are the mean and standard deviation of the historical head record, and α is calibrated to match stress-strain diagram classification. When head h ≤ h*, subsidence is inelastic; when h > h*, it is elastic.

Key results from 20 monitoring stations in the mid-fan and distal fan (2015–2020):

- **Inelastic proportion**: only ~15% of all monthly subsidence events are inelastic, occurring predominantly in **January–March** (the spring planting season).
- **Head thresholds** vary systematically: Neiliao (mid-fan) ~ −11 m MSL; Yiwu (distal fan) ~ −30 m MSL. The lower (more negative) threshold in the distal fan reflects historical over-compaction that has permanently lowered the preconsolidation head.
- **Spatial pattern**: the threshold follows a **northwest–southeast gradient** — higher in the northwest (coarser sediments, less compaction history), lower in the southeast (finer sediments, more past compaction).
- **Six-year unrecoverable subsidence** (2015–2020): maximum 13.4–13.9 cm, spatial extent of >11 cm unrecoverable subsidence ≈ 63 km²; annual unrecoverable maximum ≈ 2.5 cm/yr.

This rule provides an operational tool: groundwater managers can restrict pumping when head approaches h* at a given location, without requiring real-time subsidence monitoring at every well site.

## 8. Mitigation Strategies and Management Implications

### 8.1 Reduction of Pumping in Confined Aquifer Zones

The fundamental management prescription from the M13/M23 model analysis [1] is:

- **Reduce or prohibit pumping** in Model M23 zones (positive GWL–elevation correlation, distal fan), where each metre of head decline produces irreversible inelastic subsidence.
- **Pumping can continue or be relocated** to Model M13 zones (negative correlation, proximal fan), where head decline actually causes the surface to rise slightly and the response is elastic and reversible.

This insight allows spatially selective management that does not require a blanket ban on all groundwater use.

### 8.2 Mingzhu Basin as an Underground Reservoir

The Zhushan–Mingjian Basin (名竹盆地; hereafter Mingzhu Basin), located at the eastern boundary of the CRAF where the Choushui River meets the Qingshui River (清水溪), is a structural basin east of the longitudinal railway bridge and west of the Chelungpu Fault. It overlies an unconfined gravel aquifer (Model M13 zone) that recharges rapidly from both rivers [1].

Key attributes:
- Pumping from the Mingzhu Basin unconfined aquifer causes the surface to **rise slightly**, not sink.
- Estimated sustainable annual yield: **100–200 million m³/year**.
- Water can be conveyed through the Jiji Weir (集集攔河堰) north–south linking channels to supply the downstream CRAF, **displacing an equivalent volume of deep confined-aquifer pumping** in the distal fan.

Numerical model studies are needed to quantify the trade-off: reduced lateral recharge from the Mingzhu Basin to the CRAF must be more than offset by the reduction in distal-fan confined-aquifer pumping to achieve a net benefit.

### 8.3 Artificial Recharge

Artificial recharge of the CRAF confined aquifer system has been a policy goal of the WRA. If the Hefeng confined-aquifer head can be raised above −12.7 m MSL through a combination of reduced pumping and surface spreading or injection recharge, the **residual 38.8 cm of projected inelastic subsidence** would not materialise [1]. This represents the largest achievable subsidence mitigation in the distal fan under current projections.

### 8.4 Subsidence Monitoring and Early Warning

Multiple studies [3, 4, 10] have highlighted the importance of dense, multi-sensor monitoring for early detection of new subsidence centres. The THSR traverses the southern CRAF and is particularly vulnerable to differential settlement [10]. The shift of the subsidence centre from Lunbei/Baojhou (1996–1998) to Tuku/Yuanchang (post-2000) was detected only after the fact by leveling surveys; real-time GNSS and InSAR monitoring now provides near-continuous coverage.

Time-dependent spatial regression [2] offers a framework to use electricity-billing data for **near-real-time monitoring** of pumping volumes across the entire fan without requiring comprehensive physical metering of individual wells. This approach could be implemented operationally alongside existing monitoring networks.

### 8.5 Implications for Subsidence Modelling

Quantitative evaluations of pumping-induced subsidence [7] using integrated remote sensing and site-specific hydrogeological observations demonstrate that the relationship between pumping, head decline, and subsidence is highly nonlinear and spatially variable. This has direct implications for predictive models: (1) spatially uniform storage coefficients will misrepresent the inland–coastal subsidence offset; (2) elastic and inelastic compaction must be distinguished explicitly, because permanent compaction from sustained overdraft accumulates even after pumping is reduced to pre-threshold levels.

### 8.6 Next-generation monitoring: the TKJS supersite

Hung et al. [13] established a supersite at Tuku Junior High School (TKJS) in central Yunlin that integrates the highest-density multi-depth monitoring in the CRAF. The supersite comprises two monitoring areas:

**Area A** — three deep borehole extensometers at 130 m, 300 m, and 400 m depth, each using a nested-pipe configuration with an LVDT sensor and CR1000X data logger recording at **10-minute intervals**. The extensometer measurement resolution is 0.015 mm RMSE. A 400-m continuous core was extracted and analysed: 135 sediment samples show gravel (7%), medium-to-coarse sand (37%), very-fine-to-fine sand (29%), and fine-grained sediments (27%). A 400-m groundwater well monitors head at the deepest target.

**Area B** — one continuous GNSS station, a 300-m MLCW with 20 magnetic rings, one leveling benchmark, and three groundwater wells screened at 87 m, 179 m, and 263 m.

**Key data from the TKJS supersite (2015–2023):**

| Aquifer | GWL decline rate (m/yr) | Annual amplitude (m) |
|---------|:----------------------:|:-------------------:|
| Aquifer 1 (36 m) | 0.48 | 0.98 |
| Aquifer 2 (87 m) | 0.31 | 2.44 |
| Aquifer 3 (179 m) | 0.31 | 2.41 |
| Aquifer 4 (263 m) | 0.18 | 1.88 |

Shallow aquifers respond faster to seasonal recharge and pumping; deeper aquifers show slower but persistent decline. The MLCW at TKJS recorded ~25 cm cumulative compaction from December 2014 to December 2024, with principal compression at 50–156 m and 241–295 m. Comparison of matched-depth intervals between extensometer and MLCW yields an RMSE of ~1.0 cm, confirming that both technologies capture the same deformation signal at different temporal resolutions.

**AI forecasting:** A Prophet Bayesian time-series model trained on the high-frequency extensometer data achieved an RMSE of **0.34 mm for a 4-month forecast horizon** after parameter optimization — a 35% improvement over the default configuration. This demonstrates the potential for near-real-time subsidence forecasting at key monitoring sites.

## 9. Conclusion

The Choushui River Alluvial Fan presents one of the most comprehensively documented case studies of groundwater-induced land subsidence globally. Nine key conclusions emerge from this review:

1. **Seasonal GWL oscillations** of several metres are superimposed on a long-term declining trend that has depleted approximately 2.5 billion m³ of groundwater storage since 1976. Annual oscillation amplitudes increase toward the southwest distal fan.

2. **Total groundwater extraction** is approximately 240,000 m³/day (~87 million m³/year), dominated by paddy-rice irrigation. Because private wells are unmetered, pumping must be inferred from groundwater hydrograph analysis, drawdown-subsidence regression, or electricity consumption data.

3. **Electricity consumption** provides a spatially distributed, regularly updated proxy for regional pumping volumes. Time-dependent spatial regression of electricity-inferred pumping against observed subsidence achieves RMSE ~0.65 cm/year at the fan scale, enabling both retrospective analysis and predictive simulation of pumping-reduction scenarios.

4. **Land subsidence** has been as high as 12–14 cm/year in drought years (2002–2003) and has produced cumulative displacements exceeding 210 cm in the most affected townships. The total area with significant subsidence (> 3 cm/year) has been reduced from ~1,600 km² (2001) to ~800 km² (2007) following mitigation, but 4–5 cm/year subsidence persists in the Tuku–Yuanchang zone.

5. **The subsidence bowl does not spatially coincide with the pumping centre**: inelastic compaction peaks in the inland mid-fan where compressible clay aquitards are thickest, while the cone of depression extends further into the coastal distal fan.

6. **The M13/M23 interaction models** provide a physically based framework for spatially selective groundwater management: pumping can be relocated toward proximal-fan Model M13 zones (where the surface response to head decline is elastic and even slightly upward), while restricting extraction in coastal distal-fan Model M23 zones prevents further irreversible compaction.

7. **Integrated monitoring** combining MLCW compaction data, dense GNSS networks, InSAR time series, and electricity-consumption-based pumping estimation provides the necessary data foundation for operational subsidence forecasting and adaptive groundwater management across the CRAF.

8. **Statistical head thresholds** (Tatas & Chu, 2024) enable elastic/inelastic classification using only head data: ~15% of subsidence events are inelastic (concentrated in January–March), with thresholds ranging from −11 m MSL (mid-fan) to −30 m MSL (distal fan). This provides an operational rule for when to restrict pumping at unmonitored locations without requiring real-time compaction measurements.

9. **New monitoring infrastructure** at the TKJS supersite (Hung et al., 2025) — three deep extensometers at 130/300/400 m recording at 10-minute intervals, integrated with MLCW rings and a 400-m continuous core — resolves compaction at unprecedented depth and temporal resolution. Per-aquifer GWL decline rates of 0.18–0.48 m/yr (2015–2023) provide the most detailed depth-stratified head-change record in the CRAF.
