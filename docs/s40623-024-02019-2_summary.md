### 0. Metadata & Identification
- **Title:** Mechanism of vertical displacement beneath shallow compression zone in coastal area
- **Authors:** Duc‑Huy Tran, Shih‑Jung Wang, Jian‑Yu Chen
- **DOI:** 10.1186/s40623‑024‑02019‑2
- **Keywords:** land subsidence, deep displacement, hydraulic expansion, hydraulic contraction, GPS, multi‑layer compaction well, groundwater extraction, tectonic activity, poroelastic model, seasonal loading

### 1. Three‑Tiered Multi‑Level Summary
**Level 1: Layperson / Farmer**  
When the ground in a coastal area sinks (subsidence), the movement measured at the surface often differs from the movement measured in the soil layers just a few hundred meters below. This study looked at Taiwan’s Pingtung Plain and found that the deep parts of the ground move in the *opposite* direction during wet and dry seasons: when groundwater rises and the surface lifts, the deep layers actually sink a little, and vice versa. The reason is like a deep sponge: when water pressure increases in the deep layers, the sponge’s pores expand, pushing the rock above upward but loading the rock below downward (**hydraulic expansion and contraction**). This discovery helps separate natural ground movements from human‑caused sinking due to pumping, and it shows that seasonal water changes can influence earthquake activity.

**Level 2: College Student**  
Total land subsidence, observed by GPS and leveling, is separated into *shallow compression* (within the upper 200 m, monitored by multi‑layer compaction wells) and a residual termed **deep displacement**. Analysis of ten years of data from two coastal sites in Taiwan reveals that shallow compression correlates strongly with groundwater extraction, whereas deep displacement shows a *negative* correlation with groundwater levels—it declines during the wet season and uplifts during the dry season. This behavior is explained by **cyclic hydraulic loading**: seasonal water‑level changes alter pore pressure in deep aquifers, causing the overlying, low‑permeability aquitards to expand or contract. A numerical hydromechanical model (poroelastic theory) reproduces the observed reversal, demonstrating that the mechanism operates only where the deep formation’s permeability is extremely low. The contribution of deep displacement to total subsidence is about 37 % (Jiadong) and 14 % (Fangliao).

**Level 3: Graduate Student**  
The study employs cross‑correlation analysis of GPS‑derived total displacement, multi‑layer compaction well (MLCW) data, and groundwater levels to isolate **deep displacement**—the difference between surface settlement and shallow (< 200 m) compaction. Correlation coefficients between deep displacement and hydrological variables change sign as the depth threshold increases: from positive (shallow) to markedly negative (beyond ~190 m). The mechanism proposed, **hydraulic expansion and contraction**, is formalized via a coupled poroelastic model (COMSOL Multiphysics) with explicit Biot consolidation equations:

$$
\rho_f S \frac{\partial p}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot \left( \rho_f \left( -\frac{k_p}{\mu} \nabla p \right) \right) + \rho_f \alpha \frac{\partial \varepsilon_v}{\partial t} = Q_f,
$$

$$
\sigma_{ij} - \alpha p I = 0, \quad \sigma_{ij} = C_{ijkl} \varepsilon_{kl}.
$$

The model uses low‑permeability aquitard parameters (e.g., $7.50 \times 10^{-17}\,\text{m}^2$ for aquitard 3) and reproduces the strain reversal observed. The deep displacement time series exhibit a 1‑yr periodicity but with a phase opposite to that of surface displacement and groundwater level. This finding implies that tectonic assessments derived from surface geodesy without removing anthropogenic shallow compaction will carry large uncertainties, and it supports the speculation that seasonal groundwater variations may modulate seismicity. The long‑term linear trend (Jiadong: –4.6 mm yr⁻¹, Fangliao: +0.9 mm yr⁻¹) is tentatively attributed to tectonic escape, though the evidence remains insufficient.

### 2. Explicit Author Content & Research Motivation
- **Stated Research Questions:** The study aims to separate land subsidence into shallow compression and deep displacement and to determine the physical mechanism responsible for the observed deep displacement, which varies in an opposite sense to both ground‑surface movement and shallow groundwater level changes.
    - *“This study adopts two kinds of geodetic observation data and separates land subsidence into shallow compression and deep displacement to investigate the mechanism of deep displacement.”*
    - *“The mechanism is unknown and requires further investigation.”*

- **Explicit Motivations:** The discrepancy between total subsidence (measured on the ground surface) and shallow compression (measured within a few hundred meters) had been noted in Taiwan but lacked an explanatory mechanism. Understanding deep displacement is essential because surface‑based observations are contaminated by anthropogenic shallow signals, causing large uncertainty in tectonophysical studies.
    - *“Previous studies … showed that there is a discrepancy between total subsidence … and shallow compression … in Taiwan. This difference is caused by displacement beyond the major compression zone, which is referred to as deep displacement in this study.”*
    - *“The influence of anthropogenic activity can have a large effect on the investigation of mechanisms in tectonophysical studies.”*

- **The Gap / Consequence:** Without this research, the mechanism of the anti‑correlated deep displacement remains unknown, preventing any correction of surface geodetic data for deep natural signals and leaving tectonic assessments biased by anthropogenic shallow effects. Current literature proposed deep compression due to pumping, but observed variations contradict that hypothesis.
    - *“However, the variations of deep displacement are opposite to those of groundwater level variations at shallow depths … which is inconsistent with deep compression due to groundwater over‑extraction.”*

- **Key Findings and Numeric Results:**
    - Deep displacement contributes 37.06 % and 13.63 % to total subsidence in Jiadong and Fangliao, respectively.
        - *“The contribution of deep displacement to total land subsidence is 37.06% and 13.63% in the Jiadong and Fangliao areas, respectively.”*
    - Average deep displacement rates are –4.6 mm yr⁻¹ (Jiadong) and +0.9 mm yr⁻¹ (Fangliao).
        - *“The average rates of deep displacement are − 4.6 and 0.9 mm/yr in the Jiadong and Fangliao areas, respectively.”*
    - Cross‑correlation coefficients between deep displacement and groundwater levels decrease and become negative as the depth threshold increases.
        - *“The results clearly show that at a certain depth, the correlation between deep displacement and hydrological observations transitions from positive to negative.”*
    - Hydromechanical model reproduces strain reversal: compression in aquifers 1–3 in dry season, dilation in aquitard 3.
        - *“The simulated strain in aquitard 3 shows the opposite responses, that is, an increasing trend (dilation) in the dry season and a decreasing trend (compression) in the wet season.”*

- **Explicit Conclusions:** The seasonal variations of deep displacement are caused by cyclic hydraulic loading and unloading, manifesting as hydraulic expansion and contraction in low‑permeability deep strata. The mechanism explains the anti‑correlation and supports the speculation that groundwater‑level variations can trigger earthquakes.
    - *“The study results provide important information on the mechanism of deep displacement that can be used in tectonophysical and land subsidence investigations.”*
    - *“The opposite responses indicate that the effects of natural factors … obtained without the removal of the influence of anthropogenic activity will lead to large uncertainty in the assessment results.”*

- **Stated Limitations or Caveats:**
    - Observation stations are not exactly collocated, which introduces uncertainty.
        - *“Note that the observation stations are not completely collocated. … The comparison of non‑collocated data is a source of uncertainty in this study.”*
    - The MLCW data have a gap in 2013–2014; the long‑term linear trend of deep displacement lacks direct evidence for a tectonic origin.
        - *“The MLCW data in the period 2013–2014 are missing.”*
        - *“However, there is no evidence to support this mechanism; further investigations are thus needed.”*

#### Data Preprocessing and Alignment
Weekly average of continuous GPS mid‑point solutions is computed. For each MLCW measurement date, the GPS data within ±3 days are averaged to match the monthly MLCW sampling.
- **Materials/Inputs:** Daily GPS solutions from stations CLON and FALI; monthly MLCW compaction records.
- **Verb. support:**
    - *“The weekly average from the mid‑point solution of GPS data is calculated to reduce numerical errors.”*
    - *“GPS data collected within three days before and after the reference date of MLCW data are averaged (for a total of 7 GPS data points).”*

#### Deep Displacement Calculation
At each MLCW time stamp, the averaged GPS vertical displacement (total surface movement) is subtracted by the MLCW compaction summed from the surface to the maximum MLCW depth (∼200 m). The difference is defined as deep displacement.
- **Materials/Inputs:** Time‑series of total vertical displacement (GPS) and shallow compaction (MLCW).
- **Verb. support:**
    - *“The MLCW data obtained at the same time point are subtracted from the averaged results of GPS data to calculate their difference (i.e., the deep displacement).”*

#### Cross‑correlation and Depth‑threshold Analysis
Pearson correlation coefficients between deep displacement and groundwater levels, rainfall, etc., are computed. Additionally, the threshold separating shallow and deep components is varied (e.g., 47 m, 118 m, 194 m in Jiadong), and the correlation of the resulting “deep” signal with hydrological data is examined.
- **Materials/Inputs:** Deep displacement time series (at different cutoff depths), groundwater level time series (wells DZ, WF, DX, FL, DeX), cumulative rainfall departure (CRD).
- **Verb. support:**
    - *“The results of cross‑correlation are listed in Table 3. … The results clearly show that at a certain depth, the correlation between deep displacement and hydrological observations transitions from positive to negative.”*

#### Hydromechanical Simulation (Poroelastic Model)
A 2D profile with three aquifers and three aquitards (total depth 200 m, extended 2.5 km laterally) is built in COMSOL Multiphysics. The model solves Biot’s poroelastic equations. Boundary conditions: rollers on lateral sides, fixed base, free‑traction top. Seasonal hydraulic head variations (dry/wet cycles) are applied as source/sink in the aquifers.
- **Materials/Inputs:** Hydrogeological geometry (Fig. 5), material properties from Table 1 (permeability, Young’s modulus, Poisson ratio, Biot coefficient, etc.), seasonal hydraulic head changes from DeX well.
- **Formulas & Parameters:**
  Governing fluid mass conservation:
  $$\rho_f S\frac{\partial p}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot\left(\rho_f\left(-\frac{k_p}{\mu}\nabla p\right)\right) + \rho_f\alpha\frac{\partial\varepsilon_v}{\partial t} = Q_f$$
  Stress equilibrium and linear elasticity:
  $$\begin{cases}
  \sigma_{ij} - \alpha p I = 0,\\
  \sigma_{ij} = C_{ijkl}\varepsilon_{kl},\quad
  C_{ijkl} = \frac{E\nu}{(1+\nu)(1-2\nu)}\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl} + \frac{E}{2(1+\nu)}\left(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}\right)
  \end{cases}$$
  Strain tensor: $\varepsilon_{ij} = \frac{1}{2}\left(\nabla u + (\nabla u)^T\right)$.
  Key input parameters (from Table 1): Aquitard 3 permeability $7.50\times10^{-17}\,\text{m}^2$, Young’s modulus $4.56\times10^7\,\text{Pa}$, Poisson ratio 0.4.

### 5. Study Area & Setting (Contextual Analysis)
- **Location:** Coastal area of Pingtung Plain, southwestern Taiwan, stretching from the proximal fan (Fangliao) to the distal fan (Jiadong) near the Linbian River.
- **Physical Characteristics:** Holocene unconsolidated sediments form a basin within a depth of ~120 m, underlain by cemented strata. The alluvial fan system exhibits significant hydrofacies variability; groundwater levels in the three aquifers fluctuate seasonally by 7–33 m. The area is bound by the Chaochou active fault, with tectonic escape directed toward the southwest (azimuth ~244°). The proximal fan has coarser sediments and a higher subsidence gradient than the distal fan.
- **The “Why”:** The region has undergone extreme cumulative subsidence (3.56 m from 1972–2018) largely due to groundwater over‑extraction, yet it sits in a tectonically active corridor. The coexistence of dense monitoring networks (GPS, MLCWs, groundwater wells) and the clear separation between shallow compression and deep tectonic signals make it an ideal natural laboratory to isolate and explain the mechanism of deep displacement.

### 6. Scientific Insights & Physical Significance (The “Impact”)
- **Primary Findings:** Seasonal groundwater recharge/discharge creates cyclic hydraulic loading that does **not** simply compress deep strata; instead, pore‑pressure increases in deep confined aquifers cause the intervening low‑permeability aquitards to expand (wet season) and contract (dry season). This **hydraulic expansion and contraction** produces deep vertical displacement that is **opposite in phase** to surface motion and shallow groundwater levels, with correlation coefficients transitioning from positive (~0.7) to negative (ca. –0.35) as the depth threshold increases beyond ~190 m.
    - *“The variations of deep displacement are opposite to those of ground surface displacement. … The hydraulic expansion and contraction can well explain the observations.”*  
    - *“The results clearly show that at a certain depth, the correlation between deep displacement and hydrological observations transitions from positive to negative.”*
- **The “So‑What”:** Because deep and shallow displacements respond oppositely to seasonal hydrology, any derivation of tectonic vertical rates from raw surface geodesy without removing shallow anthropogenic compaction will be contaminated. The documented mechanism explains why earlier attempts to interpret deep compression as pumping‑induced consolidation failed, and it supports the emerging idea that seasonal pore‑pressure variations can modulate crustal stress and influence earthquake occurrence.
    - *“The opposite responses indicate that the effects of natural factors … obtained without the removal of the influence of anthropogenic activity will lead to large uncertainty in the assessment results.”*  
    - *“This reversed phenomenon is the cyclic hydraulic expansion and contraction in the aquifer system proposed in this study.”*
- **Scientific Significance:** The work provides a physically grounded, poroelastically consistent explanation for the anti‑phased deep displacement observed in Taiwan and potentially in other tectonically active coastal basins. It establishes that **low‑permeability aquitards** serve as stress‑absorbing interfaces that invert the strain response relative to adjacent aquifers. This insight refines the interpretation of geodetic time series in subsiding deltas and offers a partial mechanism for hydrological triggering of seismicity.
