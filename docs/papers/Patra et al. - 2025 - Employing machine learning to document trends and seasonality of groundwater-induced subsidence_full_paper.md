## Abstract

Land subsidence is largely influenced by the declining trend and seasonal fluctuations of groundwater levels. This study proposes a comprehensive data-driven subsidence trend identification and forecasting system based on hydraulic head observations by integrating seasonality and trend decomposition with machine learning. The study firstly identifies the decomposition components of the deformation and hydraulic head time series data, and formulates a relationship between subsidence trend and hydraulic head to detect regions suffering from significant subsidence. Overall deformation was estimated using head changes firstly, and then subsidence trend was identified after removal of the non-trend component. Results revealed that the deformation varied with seasonal groundwater fluctuations that were dominated by a strong response during wet and dry seasons. Seasonality between subsidence and groundwater change is strongly correlated. The subsidence trend can be determined from the removal of the seasonality related to head change, especially near the proximal and mid-fan areas. A subsidence hotspot with a significantly steeper trend (> 4 cm/year) was observed in inland areas (mid-fan). The \(R^2\) achieved a strong fit when model estimation was compared with the observed deformation trend for the testing period (2019–20). The subsidence forecasting system developed in this study could enhance subsidence and groundwater management to offer the potential of identifying areas experiencing increased irrecoverable subsidence by analyzing the estimated subsidence trend associated with groundwater changes.

**Keywords:** Groundwater · Land subsidence forecasting · Seasonality and trend decomposition · Machine learning

## 1 Introduction

Land subsidence has risen to be a global environmental phenomenon because of extreme and vastly unregulated groundwater exploitation. Groundwater overexploitation critically affects the natural system that exists within underground aquifers once the threshold is crossed, thereby causing irrecoverable deformation (Smith and Majumdar 2020; Guzy and Malinowska 2020; Chu et al. 2021b; Hung et al. 2021; Tatas and Chu 2024). Subsidence poses serious threats to the environment, infrastructure, human life, and socio-economy, hence holding the potential for severe disasters, such as floods and land degradation due to aquifer salinization because of seawater intrusion in the coastal regions (Faunt et al. 2016; Bagheri-Gavkosh et al. 2021; Shirzaei et al. 2021). Various sensors, including GPS (Tangdamrongsub et al. 2019; Ali et al. 2021), wells equipped with sensors measuring soil compaction i.e., multi-level compaction wells (Hung et al. 2010, 2021; Wang et al. 2015; Ku et al. 2022; Ku and Liu 2023), leveling networks (Ali et al. 2020; Chu et al. 2021a, b), interferometric synthetic aperture radar (Hoffmann et al. 2001; Chen et al. 2020, 2021; Rateb and Abotalib 2020; Li et al. 2021; Ahmed et al. 2024), and settlement gauge (Antonellini et al. 2019), are adopted to examine subsidence rates. The modeling of subsidence commonly includes a data-driven function that can strongly represent the subsidence phenomenon for the area under investigation. Conventional studies relied on physical or numerical models for simulating land subsidence due to groundwater overexploitation (Wu et al. 2009; Wang et al. 2015; Guzy and Witkowski 2021; Chen et al. 2023). Although recent developments have purely focused on highly efficient data-driven techniques such as spatial regression and machine learning models that require intensive data records for estimation and prediction (Ali et al. 2020; Yu et al. 2020; Chen et al. 2021; Chu et al. 2021a, b; Li et al. 2021; Tatas et al. 2023; Ku and Liu 2023). Moreover, studies have also attempted to predict the vulnerability of each location to subsidence by using statistical as well as machine learning approaches, such as random forest (RF), support vector machines, logistic regression, Bayes Net, Naïve Bayes, Logit Boost, AdaBoost, and artificial neural network (Bui et al. 2018; Mohammady et al. 2019; Oh et al. 2019; Hakim et al. 2020; Zamanirad et al. 2020; Arabameri et al. 2021). These methods are computationally efficient, and provide robust quantification on either small or big data. However, these studies have only exerted tremendous efforts in monitoring and modeling land subsidence without considering subsidence trend and seasonality identification.

Understanding groundwater varying responses, such as seasonal variation, may further help in the effective quantification of the mechanism involved within an aquifer system. Most of the studies usually derived groundwater changes at local-scales from monitoring wells (Gau et al. 2007; MacAllister et al. 2022), whereas researchers also used gravity recovery and climate experiment (GRACE) data to monitor the changes in groundwater storage at regional and global scales (Tangdamrongsub et al. 2019; Vasco et al. 2022). Such studies are usually conducted through time series trend analysis, which provides insight into the groundwater flow during the seasonal recharge and pumping events in an aquifer system (Lafare et al. 2016). The time series trend analysis is usually conducted using statistical methods, such as the Mann–Kendall test (Tabari et al. 2012; Patle et al. 2015; Ilia et al. 2018; Xing et al. 2018; Guo et al. 2019, 2020; Tsangaratos et al. 2019; Satish Kumar and Venkata Rathnam 2019). However, such classical techniques are most efficient at detecting monotonic behaviors in the time series and are adversely affected by seasonality. Hence, their usefulness is limiting where seasonality changes over time (Shamsudduha et al. 2009). A time series decomposition into trend, seasonal, and residue enables us to fully comprehend the long-term process and identify hidden patterns within an aquifer system that will further benefit and contribute to enhanced modeling through clear signals for trend predictions. Decomposition helps formulate a functional relationship between the groundwater and subsidence for the elastic deformation in the aquifer system (Hoffmann et al. 2001). Various studies conducted time series decomposition only on groundwater to reveal its dynamic response for long-term monitoring. For instance, groundwater anomalies preceding seismic events were detected through an additive time series decomposition model, indicating potential earthquake precursors in Central Taiwan (Gau et al. 2007). Another study proposed time series decomposition to uncover the effects of unsustainable irrigation on shallow aquifers and seawater intrusion in coastal aquifers driven by rising sea levels in Ganges–Brahmaputra–Meghna Delta (Shamsudduha et al. 2009). Furthermore, time series decomposition was also applied to study local hydrogeological and physical characteristics in Eden Valley, UK (Lafare et al. 2016). Decomposition techniques have contributed significantly for characterization of subsidence into recoverable and irrecoverable portions. For instance, Antonellini et al. (2019) demonstrated the estimation of irrecoverable subsidence through its decomposed trend component using the analytical formulas of 1D consolidation equation (Terzaghi 1923). Rateb and Abotalib (2020) utilized the decomposition-derived trend component for inferring irrecoverable subsidence from Sentinel-1 data over the Nile delta, Africa. Chu et al. (2024) conducted empirical mode decomposition to derive major subsidence feature components such as long-term (trend), periodic (seasonal), and intra-seasonal variations that reflected anthropogenic activities and hydrogeology in Central Taiwan. Few studies have also adopted relevant decomposition approaches conjointly over satellite-derived land subsidence and hydraulic head for quantifying elastic and inelastic aquifer responses along with aquifer properties (Jiang et al. 2018; Vajedian et al. 2024). Although there is literature addressing the comprehensive decomposition of land deformation and hydraulic head data simultaneously and its integration into subsidence modeling, research specifically focused on the joint decomposition and modelling used in this study remains relatively limited. Most of the literature has not yet addressed the crucial aspects e.g., trend and seasonality conjointly by integrating them from time series decomposition technique with data-driven machine learning models for accurate subsidence estimation.

This study aims to provide such subsidence trend identification and forecasting system at a majorly affected region in Central Taiwan. Vertical deformation was primarily dominated by trend component (monotonic downward), and seasonality controlled (non-monotonic) groundwater variations. This research firstly investigates the subsidence and groundwater measurements through decomposition in the study area to solidify this hypothesis, and then highlights a sophisticated framework that extracts trend and seasonality components from time series decomposition. The subsidence trends and seasonality were estimated through groundwater observations using machine learning models. Eventually, the estimated subsidence trends are compared with decomposition-derived trend components from the original ground deformation records for evaluation.

## 2 Study area and its pertinent dataset

### 2.1 Study area

The area under investigation in this study is a portion of the Choushui River Alluvial Fan (CRAF) located in the west-central region of Taiwan (i.e., Yunlin County with an estimated area of about 1291 km²). The geographical location of the study area is depicted in Fig. 1. Two prominent surface streams are available in this region, one to the north is the Choushui River, and its southern counterpart is known as the Beigang River. The alluvial fan extends from the east (central mountain range) towards the western coast near the Taiwan Strait. The study area experiences an elevation change that ranges between 0 and 100 m from the west coast towards the proximal boundary in the east (Ali et al. 2021; Chen et al. 2021). The sediments in CRAF can be mostly ascribed to the rock formations in the upstream watershed, which comprises mudstone, metamorphic, quartzite, shale, and slate (Liu et al. 2004; Ali et al. 2020, 2021; Chu et al. 2021a). The aquifer system within the hydrogeological profile that extends near the Choushui River (see AA’ in Fig. 1) revealed four aquifers, namely, aquifer-I, II-1, II-2, III, and IV, beneath the alluvial fan (Chu et al. 2021a; Tatas et al. 2023; Patra et al. 2023). The aquitards (T1–T3) with fine-grained sediments having semi-permeable properties function as a delimiter between each aquifer (Fig. 2). These aquitards are most prevalent throughout the western coast and gradually reduce towards the east where aquifers become prominent, thereby resulting in higher rechargeability in the proximal areas (Jang et al. 2008; Yu and Chu 2010). Aquifer-II is the most dominant aquifer in this region because of its large spatial extent and high storativity; therefore, it is the primary source of water for the entire local population (Liu et al. 2004; Yu and Chu 2010; Chu et al. 2021a). The groundwater depletion in this aquifer is the leading cause of extreme subsidence in the region. Furthermore, infiltration through nearby fish ponds also transpired after subsequent groundwater pumping for aquaculture activities near the coastal areas (Liu et al. 2003).

Subsidence is critically recognized, particularly in this area, because of its increased threats to infrastructure and population, especially to the Taiwan High-Speed Railway (THSR) line that connects the western metropolises in Taiwan. The local population has been increasingly dependent on groundwater for agricultural, industrial, and domestic usage because of insufficient surface water resources. The groundwater abstraction rises exorbitantly during the dry season or drought periods (Ali et al. 2020; Chu et al. 2021b), further diminishing the hydraulic head within aquifer-II, resulting in large compaction of fine-grained sediment layers (silt and clay) ultimately causing severe subsidence at the ground level. Despite the implementation of aggressive groundwater conservation programs, including strict regulations on illegal pumping by the Water Resource Agency of Taiwan, maximum annual subsidence in 2015 still exceeded 7 cm (Chu et al. 2021b). Furthermore, recent subsidence rates surpass 4–5 cm annually in some agricultural hotspots of the central part of Yunlin County where the THSR passes through (Chen et al. 2021; Ku et al. 2022; Ku and Liu 2023).

### 2.2 Observation data

The dataset utilized in this study included hydraulic head and land deformation data acquired through an extensive field network comprising eighteen hydraulic head monitoring wells as well as multi-level compaction wells (Hung et al. 2010, 2021; Chu et al. 2021a) spread across Yunlin County (Fig. 1). The selected wells for this study were based on two conditions. First, the distance between a groundwater well and a compaction well was limited to 1 km to preserve maximum correlation and diminish any form of uncertainties between the two variables as much as possible. Second, wells with a higher degree of missing data were omitted, and wells with less than 5% of missing values were filled through linear interpolation. The deformation data were acquired at a depth of 300 m to analyze compaction at deeper levels that occurs within the hydrogeological structure while groundwater fluctuations originated from the highly exploited aquifer-II. The dataset covered a period of 6 years, that is, from 2015 to 2020, with monthly readings for hydraulic head and subsurface deformation. The established dataset from the wide network of monitoring and compaction wells over CRAF in Yunlin County is maintained, calibrated, and operated by the Water Resources Agency of Taiwan.

## 3 Methods

Directly estimating trends in deformation that represent the degree of temporal non-stationarity within the time series data is quite difficult. Therefore, estimating subsidence components considers multiple steps in our study. The workflow for estimating of subsidence trend and seasonality is illustrated in Fig. 3, which involves the integration of time series decomposition analysis and estimation through models. The time series of hydraulic head (\(h\)) and deformation (\(s\)) were first separated into three components i.e., trend, seasonality, and residue for each location through time series decomposition (Sect. 3.2) to provide further insights into the hydro-geodynamic process associated with the deformation caused by the soaring groundwater abstraction in the region. Subsequently, subsidence change computed through raw time series data and its seasonality obtained from time series decomposition was modeled and estimated from drawdown-deformation model (Sect. 3.3), and the subsidence seasonality (non-trend) model (Sect. 3.4), respectively. Eventually, the estimated subsidence trend was determined by the removal of the estimated subsidence seasonality from the overall estimated subsidence through the models.

### 3.1 Subsidence seasonality and trend estimation

Firstly, the drawdown-deformation model was trained over hydraulic head change (\(\Delta h\)) as the independent with deformation change (\(\Delta s\)) being the dependent variable (Eq 1), and was assumed that subsidence can be linearized where incremental hydraulic head change was typically small. Here, change in each variable at a particular time instance is the difference between the current measurement (\(t\)) and its predecessor (\(t-1\)) in the original time series data (Ali et al. 2021). This approach is popularly termed as the first-order differencing technique for reducing the temporal non-stationarity in time series data. The negative values (\(-\)) were regarded as subsidence/drawdown whereas the positive values (\(+\)) were referred to as uplift/rising. Subsequently, current subsidence (\(s_t\)) was determined from the addition of preceding subsidence (\(s_{t-1}\)) with the estimated current subsidence change (\(\Delta s_t\)) from SR (Eq. 2).

\[
\begin{aligned}
\Delta s_t &= f(\Delta h_t), \\
\Delta s_t &= s_t - s_{t-1}, \\
\Delta h_t &= h_t - h_{t-1}
\end{aligned}
\tag{1}
\]

\[
s_t = s_{t-1} + \Delta s_t
\tag{2}
\]

Following the time series decomposition (Sect. 3.2) of hydraulic head (\(h\)) and subsidence (\(s\)) into trend, seasonality, and residual components, the non-trend portion here included the seasonal and residual components (Cleveland et al. 1990). The non-trend subsidence (\(s_{\text{non-trend}}\)) was modeled using non-trend hydraulic head (\(h_{\text{non-trend}}\)) through RF (Eq. 3).

\[
\begin{aligned}
s_{\text{non-trend}}(t) &= f(h_{\text{non-trend}}(t)), \\
s_{\text{non-trend}}(t) &= s_{\text{seasonal}}(t) + s_{\text{residue}}(t), \\
h_{\text{non-trend}}(t) &= h_{\text{seasonal}}(t) + h_{\text{residue}}(t)
\end{aligned}
\tag{3}
\]

Finally, the value of the subsidence trend component (\(s_{\text{trend}}(t)\)) was estimated based on the overall deformation (\(s_t\)) minus the estimated non-trend deformation (\(s_{\text{non-trend}}(t)\)) at a given time (\(t\)).

\[
s_{\text{trend}}(t) = s_t - s_{\text{non-trend}}(t)
\tag{4}
\]

Since our database included only six years of monthly data, we proposed to utilize the first 4 years (i.e., 2015–2018) as the training period for model calibration (864 data from 48 monthly observations in 18 locations were used), whereas the two remaining years (i.e., 2019–2020) were spent for testing and evaluating the models (432 data).

### 3.2 Seasonality and trend decomposition

Decomposition techniques were proposed to understand the underlying process within a time series. The classical decomposition technique assumed the seasonal component to be constant throughout the adopted period, which is quite unrealistic during climate change. Hence, the more advanced seasonality and trend decomposition (STD) procedure based on LOESS (Cleveland et al. 1990) that stands for locally weighted regression (Cleveland 1979) was proposed and was later modified (Cleveland and Devlin 1988). Such modifications made STD non-parametric, which is highly efficient in detecting non-linear patterns within a time series data with seasonal variations (Shamsudduha et al. 2009; Lafare et al. 2016). STD only accounts for additive decomposition, in which time series is assumed to be a sum of all three components (i.e., trend, seasonal, and residue). In this study, the STD was utilized with an established mathematical expression (Cleveland et al. 1990), as given in Eq 5.

\[
Y_{\text{original}} = Y_{\text{trend}} + Y_{\text{seasonal}} + Y_{\text{residue}}
\tag{5}
\]

where \(Y_{\text{original}}\) is the original groundwater or deformation time series, \(Y_{\text{trend}}\) is the trend component (long-term trajectory), \(Y_{\text{seasonal}}\) is the seasonal component (repeating events), and finally \(Y_{\text{residue}}\) is the remainder or residue (short-term random events). One of the advantages of STD that requires to be highlighted is its capability to offer a dynamic seasonal component that can be modified according to parameter changes. In this study, we conducted a grid search for selecting the optimal STD parameters to efficiently capture seasonal and trend components of groundwater and deformation over each well individually. Only the season and trend smoothing parameters were optimized during the grid search, while the rest of the parameters were kept at default. The STD technique detected the underlying trend and seasonal component reasonably for groundwater and deformation data from across all the wells using the optimal parameters provided in Appendix A.

### 3.3 Drawdown‑deformation model

In this study, the spatial regression (SR) model was used to derive the spatially varying function between groundwater (\(\Delta h\)) and deformation change (\(\Delta s\)) (Eq. 1). The traditional ordinary least square method was modified to include spatial heterogeneity in the regression model (Brunsdon et al. 1996). The SR model focuses on the estimation of a parameter matrix \(\hat{\beta}(u_i, v_i)\) at location \(i\) with \(u\) and \(v\) coordinates to fit \(\Delta s_t\) value. Since subsurface deformation can vary significantly based on local geological and hydrogeological factors, such as soil properties and aquifer conditions, we employed the SR model which allows the coefficients (\(\hat{\beta}(u_i, v_i)\)) governing the groundwater-subsidence relationship to vary spatially across locations. This approach captures the localized effects of soil compaction in response to groundwater changes, providing a spatially-varying representation of subsidence patterns across different regions. The Eq. 1 can be modified in the context of SR model given as Eq. 6:

\[
\Delta s_{i,t} = \beta_0(u_i, v_i) + \hat{\beta}(u_i, v_i) \Delta h_{i,t}
\tag{6}
\]

where

\[
\hat{\beta}(u_i, v_i) = [H^T W(u_i, v_i) H]^{-1} H^T W(u_i, v_i) S
\tag{7}
\]

\[
W(u_i, v_i) = \text{diag}(w_1(i), w_2(i), \dots, w_j(i))
\tag{8}
\]

\[
w_j(i) = \exp\left(-\left(\frac{d_{ij}}{b}\right)^2\right)
\tag{9}
\]

\[
S = \begin{bmatrix} \Delta s_{1,t} \\ \vdots \\ \Delta s_{m,t} \end{bmatrix}, \quad 
H = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & \Delta h_{1,t} \\ \vdots & \vdots \\ 1 & \Delta h_{m,t} \end{bmatrix}
\tag{10}
\]

where \(S\) is the matrix for deformation change and \(H\) is the matrix of the independent variable e.g., groundwater change; \(\beta_0(u_i, v_i)\) and \(\hat{\beta}(u_i, v_i)\) are the intercepts and slope coefficients in the SR, respectively; \(W(u_i, v_i)\) is the spatial weight matrix based on the Gaussian decay function (Eq. 9); \(d_{ij}\) is the Euclidean distance between observations at \(i\) and \(j\); \(b\) represents a non-negative parameter referred to as bandwidth, 0.391 here; \(m\) is total number of considered wells. The primary objective of this model is to determine an optimum bandwidth on which the kernel function along with its associated spatial weight is computed. The optimal bandwidth is selected through cross-validation based on golden section search, in which the error between observed and estimated values are targeted to be minimized as much as possible. In this study, the SR function was implemented on the MATLAB interface (LeSage and Pace 2009).

### 3.4 Subsidence non‑trend model

Non-trend relation between subsidence and groundwater (Eq 3) is modeled using Random Forest (RF) that is a direct extension of the decision tree which ensembles them for performing robust models. Its concepts were first introduced in 1995 (Tin Kam Ho 1995) and were further extended in 2001 (Breiman 2001). Each tree is generated through the random subsampling of the original data using a technique known as bootstrapping. The split at each node is randomly selected through bagging, which reduces variances at each split, thereby making it much more robust to outliers, overfitting, and more efficient over datasets with non-linearity (Hussein et al. 2020; Smith and Majumdar 2020; Zamanirad et al. 2020; Arabameri et al. 2021).

During the training process, the algorithm is designed to minimize the loss function (Mean Squared Error) to provide a more reliable estimate of the seasonality in deformation. Only the main parameters were optimized to control on overfitting and underfitting by observing error scores in training and testing data. The hyperparameter optimization includes the maximum depth of each tree (max_depth), minimum data size allowed in a leaf node (min_samples_leaf), number of decision trees (n_estimators), subsample for each tree construction (max_samples), and finally the seed (random_state). In this study, the RF algorithm was implemented through the scikit-learn package available for Python. Table 3 shows parameter values of the RF models.

## 4 Results

### 4.1 Seasonality and trend decomposition of groundwater and deformation

Figures 4 and 5 illustrate the time series decomposition of groundwater and deformation at coastal wells-2 and 4, respectively. The trend component of the coastal deformation time series at Well-2 (Fig. 4b) shows a mild trend of subsidence in 2017 and 2020, but displays a sign of minor stabilization in 2015 and 2018, exhibiting a strong response from the groundwater recovery (Fig. 4a) but continues on a slow downward track thereafter. Based on the land use information in the area (Ku et al. 2022; Ku and Liu 2023), locations near Well-2 and 4 are predominantly used for aquaculture. Moreover, these locations have shown evidence of infiltration from other sources such as fish ponds when groundwater is pumped out (Liu et al. 2003) which could partially explain the residual components for hydraulic head at these locations. Furthermore, the overall pumping amount in these locations were relatively low (Tseng et al. 2024). Such conditions restricted the maximum subsidence within 2.5–3.5 cm in these locations. The trend component for wells in the Central Yunlin, such as Well 17 and 18 (Figs. 6, 7), which are reportedly located in the subsidence hotspot area (Ali et al. 2020; Chu et al. 2021a, b; Ku et al. 2022; Ku and Liu 2023), exhibited a strong descending behavior instigating serious concerns to the local community as a result of excessive groundwater overdraft. These regions are principally covered with agricultural fields (70–100%) and a high proportion of fine-grained soil (81–100%) (Ku et al. 2022; Ku and Liu 2023). Moreover, pumping amount here exceeds \(6 \times 10^6\) m³ causing the highest accumulated pumping-induced drawdown in groundwater across Yunlin County (Tseng et al. 2024). Thereby, it causes exorbitant levels of subsidence exceeding 22 cm during these years (Figs. 6, 7). On the other hand, Figs. 8 and 9 provides the decomposition of groundwater and deformation data at Well 10 and 13 near the Beigang river. The deformations in these wells (Fig. 8b and 9b) showed a slight uplift during the second half (Jul–Sep) of 2019, which is usually attributed to the wet season in the study area. This phenomenon marginally improved the influence of seasonal recharge in deformation time series. The overall deformation in Well-10 remained relatively small (4 cm) due to less presence of fine-grained sediments in contrast to Well-13 (9 cm) (Ku et al. 2022; Ku and Liu 2023). Moreover, pumping in these areas remained fairly limited and the local groundwater has limited impact from pumping-induced drawdown (Tseng et al. 2024). The decomposition technique was found to be useful to separate and extract seasonal components from subsidence and groundwater time series, revealing the elastic deformation portion due to seasonal change in groundwater. Evidently, the seasonal portions from subsidence and groundwater are highly correlated (\(r > 0.8\)) across most locations (Table 1). Overall, the decomposition of the hydraulic head time series revealed that a fair bit of replenishment has occurred following the subsequent drawdowns during the 6 years, thereby resulting in a non-monotonic behavior from its trend component. On the contrary, a clear monotonic trend that primarily moved downwards throughout the period was observed for the deformation time series data in most of the wells, which is particularly indicative of strong irrecoverable (inelastic) subsidence (Antonellini et al. 2019; Rateb and Abotalib 2020), especially over most of the agricultural hotspots with high pumping. This inconsistency can be further evidenced through weak correlations observed between the trend of hydraulic head and deformation where \(r\) is mostly limited below 0.4 over most of the wells (Table 1). Such a distinct pattern in trend from major decomposed components of a hydrogeologic structure increases the complexity of predictive modeling, as clearly evidenced by the previously provided stress–strain curve (Hung et al. 2021; Tatas and Chu 2024), which is indicative of a non-linear relationship between groundwater and ground deformation over majority of the location.

### 4.2 Model performance on deformation estimation

Models for deformation estimation were evaluated based on the coefficient of determination (\(R^2\)), root mean square error (RMSE), and mean absolute error (MAE) to check model generalization (Zamanirad et al. 2020). Table 2 provides the evaluation metrics obtained by the SR model (Eq 6) for the estimation of deformation change (\(\Delta s\)) through groundwater change (\(\Delta h\)) as an independent variable. The SR model achieved an overall RMSE of 0.315 cm, whereas the MAE stood at 0.218 cm during the training stage (2015–18). As for the validation period (2019–20), the model attained an RMSE of 0.266 cm and MAE of 0.2 cm. When compared with the observed values, the model estimations also exhibited a strong \(R^2\) value of 0.836 and 0.883 (Fig. 10) during the training and testing periods, respectively. Such low differences among evaluation metrics over two independent sets of data indicate superior learning/fitting capability and power/generalization capacity (Shi et al. 2020; Arabameri et al. 2021). Such precise estimations in deformation change through hydraulic head change can be ascribed to their strong correlation, as previously discussed in various studies conducted over CRAF (Liu et al. 2004; Hung et al. 2010, 2021; Yu and Chu 2010; Ali et al. 2020, 2021; Chen et al. 2021; Chu et al. 2021a, b), since, subsidence persistently happened if the groundwater drawdown exceeded a threshold (Hung et al. 2021; Tatas and Chu 2024).

Correspondingly, a similarly good performance was witnessed on the RF model over the cluster of wells (Fig. 14) in estimating the non-trend deformation time-series data (\(s_{\text{non-trend}}\)) (Table 2). Based on the results, the non-trend models can be considered using a single RF or two RFs for cluster-1 and cluster-2 (Fig. 14). The clustered machine learning model yielded a slightly higher performance when compared to a single RF model. Although both the single RF or two RFs models are feasible to generate subsidence estimation effectively. The predictive performance here exhibited no signs of overfitting when comparing RMSE and MAE which had less variance on the two data subsets (i.e., training (2015–18) & testing (2019–20)) that attribute to strong parameter optimization during the training stage (optimal RF parameter values are listed in Appendix B). Seasonal elastic deformations from intra-annual fluctuation in hydraulic head occurred between dry and wet seasons. Seasonal subsidence is related to seasonal groundwater variations which is associated with changes in wet-dry condition and agriculture pumping as evidenced through high correlation amongst them (Table 1). After the removal of trend and seasonality from the time series, the residue still contains crucial information about the hydrogeologic response with short-term variations or uncertainty. The proposed RF model considered non-trend portions (seasonality + residue) from hydraulic head and deformation time series. Such modeling through machine learning was the optimum choice because of the inclusion of residue, which represents non-linear short-term events into the seasonal component (Lafare et al. 2016). The integrated decomposition and modeling approach proposed here offers a reliable representation of the groundwater and subsidence trend and seasonal changes.

### 4.3 Subsidence trend identification and spatial mapping

The estimated deformation (\(s_t\)) starting from January 2015 until December 2020 was computed using the predicted values of \(\Delta s\) from SR (Eqs. 1 and 2). The estimated monthly deformation (\(s_t\)) were then subtracted with the corresponding estimation value of non-trend deformation (\(s_{\text{non-trend}}(t)\)) from the RF model for obtaining the deformation trend component (\(s_{\text{trend}}(t)\)) at each well (Eq. 4). The estimated subsidence trend component was validated through comparison with the original deformation time-series data from the compaction wells (Fig. 11). We provided the visualization at four wells across Yunlin County with spatially varying geography, geomorphology, geologic settings, and land use. The deformation trend across all four wells was mostly negative which refers to irrecoverable subsidence. The well in Fig. 11a is located in a coastal area with high aquaculture activity (Ku et al. 2022; Ku and Liu 2023) rigorously influenced by rapid fluctuations in groundwater and infiltration from nearby fish ponds (Liu et al. 2003), thereby making it a much more complex system for modeling. However, the model demonstrated in this study showed promising results over the period. Satisfactorily, the trend component during 2015–20 matches the overall direction of the original compaction well data. Similarly, the plot in Fig. 11b, which is also located along the southwest coastline, shows an identical pattern during the six-year period for both training (2015–2018) and testing (2019–2020) set although with slightly higher subsidence due to high drawdown (Ali et al. 2022). In Fig. 11c, the well is located in an agricultural hub (Central Yunlin) with soaring concerns of subsidence in the past (Ali et al. 2020; Ku et al. 2022; Tatas et al. 2023; Ku and Liu 2023), especially to a nearby high-speed rail as a consequence of increased groundwater pumping (Tatas et al. 2023; Tseng et al. 2024). Based on our estimation, the relevant mitigation strategies have still been ineffective in controlling the deep compaction within the aquifer system, which can further induce subsidence at the ground surface. Such a strong trend indicates that the majority of the deformation is dominated by irrecoverable subsidence (Antonellini et al. 2019; Rateb and Abotalib 2020). The trend component estimated in the well (Fig. 11d) located further inland near proximal fan also implicates modest conditions in these areas, especially during 2019 and 2020.

> **Figure 11:** Modelled (2015–18) and forecasted (2019–20) trend and deformation observations (unit: cm) at compaction wells

The forecasted trends over the entire study region can be summarized through their slopes i.e., the deformation rate (cm/year) (negative: subsidence; positive: uplift). The IDW interpolation technique was adopted to visualize the forecasted trends with a spatial resolution of 0.15 km × 0.15 km during the testing period (2019–20) across Yunlin County (Fig. 12). A negative slope here indicates a downward direction, which mostly signifies presence of irrecoverable subsidence within the aquifer system. Overall, most of the region in this case study has experienced inelastic deformation throughout the years. In particular, the coastal region experienced a significantly slower rate of inelastic subsidence that ranges from approximately 0.88–1 cm/year when compared with inland areas, since these regions are particularly used for aquaculture activities with relatively low level of pumping (Ku et al. 2022; Ku and Liu 2023; Tseng et al. 2024). The highest rate of inelastic subsidence (up to 5.16 cm/year) was witnessed in agricultural areas located in the red hotspot (Central Yunlin) by 2020. These findings are relatively close to previous claims showing recent maximum annual subsidence rate in Central Yunlin area to be exceeding 5 cm (Ku and Liu 2023; Hung et al. 2024; Tseng et al. 2024). A moderate trend was witnessed just out of the hotspot areas that currently have slopes ranging between 1.5 and 2 cm/year. The map depicted in Fig. 12 closely matches the subsidence maps, especially the subsidence bowl (red) area (Central Yunlin) produced in recent research works through other sensors for surface deformation measurements such as leveling stations (Ali et al. 2020; Chu et al. 2021b; Tatas et al. 2023), InSAR (Chen et al. 2021; Ali et al. 2022), and GNSS (Hung et al. 2024). The subsidence trend shows a sufficient agreement between compaction well observations and the estimation in this study. The proposed decomposition-based subsidence trend forecasting approach in this study provides vulnerable areas especially to irrecoverable subsidence for improved groundwater and subsidence mitigation management.

## 5 Discussion

### 5.1 Trend and seasonality of subsidence

Groundwater changes typically follow cyclical trend and seasonal patterns due to variations of rainfall and water usage. However, subsurface deformation processes are primarily characterized by groundwater changes mostly corresponding to the local hydrogeological information, land use and pumping conditions. The groundwater and deformation data exhibit distinct trends patterns that pose significant difficulties for spatio-temporal analysis. This study showed that the trend component from the deformation data is quite useful and crucial for inferring irrecoverable segments in subsidence. An overall STD-derived observed deformation trend and seasonality for the testing period (2019–20) achieved an \(R^2\) value of 0.991 and 0.860 (Fig. 13), respectively, which indicates a strong association between the observed and forecasted deformation trend and seasonality. A concurrent time series decomposition over groundwater and land deformation shows that additional information can be inferred and applied for an effective data-driven forecast without developing a more time-consuming and computationally complex numerical models (Wu et al. 2009; Wang et al. 2015; Guzy and Witkowski 2021; Chen et al. 2023). Our subsidence results are consistent when compared to previous studies, especially considering numerical models. Moreover, the proposed forecasting system incorporates hydrological trend and seasonal change from hydraulic head into deformation trend and seasonality estimations through time series decomposition in drawing out intricate relationship between groundwater and the deformation data by integrating it with highly efficient SR and RF models. Such decomposition-based modeling for subsidence in conjunction with groundwater could yield elastic compressibility in the aquifer system that is crucial for sustainable groundwater management (Hoffmann et al. 2001; Chu et al. 2024). The trend component from groundwater time series shows non-monotonic behavior which implies domination of hydrological cycle. In contrast, the deformation readings are majorly monotonic dominated by a downward trend making their relationship complex and non-linear in nature. This inflicts complexity to the development of the relationship and modeling of groundwater and deformation based on raw data, as both time series exhibit significantly contrasting trend patterns. The approach proposed in this study bypasses this issue by firstly decomposing the raw time series measurements and then conducting a separate machine learning model for the changes and decomposed components. This approach allowed us to appropriately model the subsidence trend component and to clearly assess the influence of groundwater changes. Since overexploitation of groundwater largely causes subsidence which is sometimes inelastic in nature, previous studies (Antonellini et al. 2019; Rateb and Abotalib 2020) mainly focused on the inference of irrecoverable subsidence through the trend component of vertical deformation while ignoring its relationship with groundwater. In this context, studies have showed that conjunctive decomposition of groundwater and subsidence would be beneficial and further enhance the understanding of aquifer properties as well as effective quantification of its elastic and inelastic responses (Jiang et al. 2018; Vajedian et al. 2024). However, these approaches have not yet been applied to the current study area, including the integration of machine learning with decomposition for hydraulic head-based subsidence modeling. This study proposes a hydraulic head-driven subsidence trend identification approach through synergistic integration of time series decomposition and machine learning methods to enhance the overall estimation of vertical deformation while delineating areas with inelastic deformation by purely predicting hydraulic head-driven subsidence trend for effective sustainable groundwater resources and subsidence mitigation management.

### 5.2 Study limitations and future scope

Certain model limitations are acknowledged here, paving the way for future research opportunities. For example, land use, aquifer thickness, clay thickness, soil properties, lithology and tectonic activity play a crucial role in the groundwater-subsidence process, which can be incorporated as input variables to further enhance the modeling performance in a future study. The proposed models also assumed an immediate effect between groundwater drawdown and land subsidence. These processes are sometimes delayed in nature for various regions. Although a sufficient amount of instantaneous subsidence occurs due to head changes in the study area (Ali et al. 2021), the estimations from these models are currently reliable. The findings of this study align with the spatial distribution of subsidence, indicating that the average subsidence rate in the hotspot area of Central Yunlin exceeds 5 cm/year, which is consistent with previously reported assessments (Ali et al. 2021; Chen et al. 2021; Ku and Liu 2023; Hung et al. 2024; Tseng et al. 2024). Additionally, the monitoring network incorporated in this study did not include many wells over the proximal fan; hence, uncertainties still exist in this part of the region. The limitations of this approach further include inapplicability over areas with a scarce or sparse network of observation wells, and dependency on the spatio-temporal resolution of the data. Furthermore, machine learning approaches for time series prediction cannot make predictions outside the range of the training data. Although the proposed RF model only incorporates the non-trend component (seasonal + residue), this can help to avoid prediction from falling outside the expected range. In the current study, hydraulic head-driven assessment of subsidence only focused on groundwater fluctuations from confined aquifer II and deformation from the deepest layer at 300 m. Future studies could strive to perform more indepth analysis of subsidence based on groundwater information from other aquifers (I, III, and IV) while simultaneously considering compaction at multiple depths. In addition, climate variables such as rainfall and temperature also effect the groundwater variations as well as indirectly influence pumping demands (Gurdak 2017; Bhattarai et al. 2023). Climate change impacts over subsidence can also be investigated in the future study.

## 6 Conclusion

A comprehensive data-driven framework for subsidence trend and seasonality estimation was demonstrated to identify and forecast deformation trends calibrated through decomposed groundwater observation over the Choushui River Alluvial Fan in Yunlin County. The seasonality and trend separation through seasonality and trend decomposition (STD) technique was conducted over the hydraulic head and deformation time series data conjointly to investigate the hydrogeologic process. Seasonal correlations were identified to further establish the understanding of the seasonality components of subsidence and groundwater changes. Vertical deformation was majorly dominated by the trend component (monotonic downward) from groundwater drawdown and the non-monotonic component were related to seasonal groundwater variations during wet and dry seasons.

The novel contributions of this work are as follows. Firstly, the STD is used to quickly and easily extract and view trends and seasonality of deformation and groundwater data simultaneously. This revealed that the seasonality between subsidence and groundwater change is highly correlated in this aquifer. Second, the drawdown-deformation and non-trend sub-models can be utilized concurrently to model the associated overall deformation and its seasonality based on head changes, and to estimate subsidence trends that yielded a reasonable agreement with the actual deformation data (RMSE < 0.35 cm). Third, the estimated map was used to visualize the strength of the forecasted subsidence trend (> 4 cm/year) over Yunlin County. The models developed in this study were effectively calibrated and can reliably forecast subsidence using hydraulic heads as inputs. Overall, the proposed forecasting system in this study is highly capable of providing ample information on land subsidence and groundwater dynamics while further improving the interpretation of the subsidence process. Deformation change was estimated from a drawdown deformation function, and the non-trend component was estimated from the machine learning approach. Eventually, the subsidence trend was estimated from the removal of the estimated seasonality related to groundwater change. This approach achieved a high \(R^2\) value when compared with the observed deformation trend for the testing period. Results in this study illustrated a subsidence bowl in the central part of the study area similar to those in previous studies. This area suffers high irrecoverable subsidence because of excessive groundwater pumping since the estimated deformation trends here showed a steeper slope. Overall, the subsidence estimation capabilities demonstrated in this study can be relevant for the development of improved mitigation policies, sustainable groundwater, and water resource management, or further academic research in the field of hazard and environmental studies.
