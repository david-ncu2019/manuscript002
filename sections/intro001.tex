Groundwater withdrawal can cause permanent land subsidence in alluvial aquifer systems. A decline in hydraulic head reduces pore-water pressure and transfers a greater share of the overburden load to the sediment skeleton. The resulting increase in effective stress compacts susceptible aquitards and fine-grained interbeds, particularly when the stress exceeds the preconsolidation stress \citep{galloway_land_1999, herrera-garcia_mapping_2021}. Compaction at depth lowers the land surface and can increase flood exposure, alter drainage gradients, and damage infrastructure. Assessment of subsidence therefore requires information about both surface displacement and deformation within the aquifer system.

Common monitoring methods describe different parts of this deformation process. Spirit leveling and continuous Global Navigation Satellite System (cGNSS) stations measure vertical displacement at the land surface. Borehole extensometers instead measure changes in aquifer-system thickness at selected locations \citep{poland_guidebook_1984, hung2012_mlcw}. Surface displacement integrates deformation over the underlying profile and cannot identify the depth intervals responsible for the observed motion. Depth-resolved measurements are therefore needed to determine where compaction occurs.

Kết luận của đoạn là quan trắc bề mặt không cho biết lún xảy ra ở tầng nào, nên cần thiết bị đo theo độ sâu. Đoạn này chuẩn bị lý do để đoạn kế tiếp giới thiệu MLCW như công cụ đáp ứng nhu cầu đó. %

Multilayer compaction monitoring wells (MLCWs) provide such measurements by recording relative displacement between magnetic anchor rings installed at different depths \citep{hung_measuring_2021}. An MLCW can separate the deformation of several aquifer and aquitard intervals within one borehole. Depth-resolved information from an MLCW is not always available when it is needed. First, field measurements must be collected, processed, and released before they can support an assessment; this study evaluates a six-month delay of this kind, which would leave the most recent depth-resolved deformation unknown even if measurement continued throughout the delay. Second, the measurements themselves can be collected on a coarser schedule, once every 6 or 12 months instead of monthly. A further, limiting case in which no subsequent MLCW measurements become available at all is considered later in this study.

Hydraulic head and cGNSS observations provide monthly information during each of these conditions of MLCW availability. Hydraulic head changes track variations in pore-water pressure that alter effective stress, while cGNSS observations record the corresponding vertical response at the surface. These two records do not replace direct MLCW measurements, but they provide contemporaneous hydraulic and surface-deformation information that could support a monthly estimate while direct MLCW observations are unavailable. Borehole lithological logs separately describe differences in sediment composition among depth intervals and provide geological context for interpreting the estimates. How well continuous observations can support such estimation, and how point error and posterior interval performance differ across these conditions, remains an open question.

Previous studies in central Taiwan combined groundwater levels, geodetic measurements, and multilayer compaction records to examine subsidence processes or simulate aquifer-system compaction \citep{hung2012_mlcw, hung_measuring_2021}. Data-driven analyses have also reconstructed missing compaction records from hydrogeological, environmental, and land-use variables \citep{liu_reconstructing_2023}. A recent single-site study used deep learning to reconstruct cumulative compaction and examine groundwater-management scenarios \citep{liu_deep_2025}. At the same Tuku site, \citet{hung2025_realtime} used a high-frequency extensometer record and Prophet, a time-series forecasting model, to forecast short-term vertical deformation, and separately compared extensometer and MLCW records at matched depths to assess agreement between the two systems. It is not yet clear how well monthly hydraulic head and cGNSS observations can maintain depth-resolved deformation information as MLCW availability changes.

The present study addressed this question at the Tuku monitoring site in Yunlin County by fitting a separate model for each of six standardized depth sections. Each model used monthly hydraulic-head observations, monthly observations of surface displacement from cGNSS, and seasonal terms constructed from the calendar date, all drawn from the same month as the estimate; the seasonal terms are not measured observations. Historical MLCW observations supplied the actual deformation values against which each monthly estimate was compared, but previous MLCW deformation increments were not included as predictor variables. The borehole lithological log was examined separately to characterize material differences among the depth sections. The assessment considered point error together with the empirical coverage and mean width of the 90\% Bayesian posterior predictive intervals. As a further, hypothetical limiting case, the study also evaluated how these estimates would behave if no subsequent MLCW observations became available, using MLCW observations withheld from the model rather than a scenario with no observations at all.

MLCW trong quá khứ chỉ dùng để đối chiếu giá trị ước tính, không đưa vào làm biến đầu vào. The analysis addressed three questions, corresponding to the three conditions of MLCW availability introduced above. First, to what extent can monthly hydraulic-head and cGNSS observations support depth-resolved deformation estimation while finalized MLCW records are temporarily delayed, and how does that performance vary among depth sections and from the first to the sixth month of each delay period? Second, how do monthly error, the discrepancy accumulated by the next MLCW measurement, and the performance of the posterior predictive interval change when MLCW is instead measured only every 6 or 12 months, following an initial record of 3, 5, or 8 years? Third, as a limiting case, how do monthly and accumulated discrepancies evolve if no subsequent MLCW measurements become available at all? The three comparisons define how monthly depth-resolved deformation estimation responds to different forms of MLCW availability at Tuku.
