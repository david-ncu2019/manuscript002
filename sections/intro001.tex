Groundwater withdrawal can cause permanent land subsidence in alluvial aquifer systems. A decline in hydraulic head reduces pore-water pressure and transfers a greater share of the overburden load to the sediment skeleton. The resulting increase in effective stress compacts susceptible aquitards and fine-grained interbeds, particularly when the stress exceeds the preconsolidation stress \citep{galloway_land_1999, herrera-garcia_mapping_2021}. Compaction at depth lowers the land surface and can increase flood exposure, alter drainage gradients, and damage infrastructure. Assessment of subsidence therefore requires information about both surface displacement and deformation within the aquifer system.

Common monitoring methods describe different parts of this deformation process. Spirit leveling and continuous Global Navigation Satellite System (cGNSS) stations measure vertical displacement at the land surface. Borehole extensometers instead measure changes in aquifer-system thickness at selected locations \citep{poland_guidebook_1984, hung2012_mlcw}. Surface displacement integrates deformation over the underlying profile and cannot identify the depth intervals responsible for the observed motion. Depth-resolved measurements are therefore needed to determine where compaction occurs.

Multilayer compaction monitoring wells (MLCWs) provide such measurements by recording relative displacement between magnetic anchor rings installed at different depths \citep{hung_measuring_2021}. An MLCW can separate the deformation of several aquifer and aquitard intervals within one borehole. However, field observations must be collected, processed, and released before they can support an assessment. A six-month delay in data delivery would leave the most recent depth-resolved compaction unknown even if measurements continued throughout the delay.

Other monitoring records can partly describe conditions during this information gap. Hydraulic head changes track variations in pore-water pressure that alter effective stress, while cGNSS observations record the corresponding vertical response at the surface. These two records do not replace direct MLCW measurements, but they provide contemporaneous hydraulic and surface-deformation information from which provisional monthly compaction can be estimated until the MLCW observations become available. Borehole lithological logs separately describe differences in sediment composition among depth intervals and provide geological context for interpreting the estimates.

Previous studies in central Taiwan combined groundwater levels, geodetic measurements, and multilayer compaction records to examine subsidence processes or simulate aquifer-system compaction \citep{hung2012_mlcw, hung_measuring_2021}. Data-driven analyses have also reconstructed missing compaction records from hydrogeological, environmental, and land-use variables \citep{liu_reconstructing_2023}. A recent single-site study used deep learning to reconstruct cumulative compaction and examine groundwater-management scenarios \citep{liu_deep_2025}. However, the available studies did not test monthly compaction estimates for standardized depth sections under repeated, temporally ordered delays in the availability of the response observations.

The present study addressed this gap at the Tuku monitoring site in Yunlin County. One independent station-specific model was developed for each of six standardized depth sections using hydraulic head and cGNSS observations from Tuku. Historical MLCW observations defined the response used for calibration and evaluation, but previous compaction values were not included as model predictors. Each monthly estimate used hydraulic head and cGNSS observations from the same month. The borehole lithological log was examined separately to characterize material differences among the depth sections. The task was therefore a provisional assessment of current compaction under delayed response observations, rather than a forecast based on unknown future hydraulic conditions.

The analysis addressed three questions. First, can contemporaneous hydraulic head and cGNSS displacement estimate monthly compaction while MLCW observations remain unavailable for six months? Second, how does estimation performance vary among depth sections and with the time since the latest model update? Third, does updating the model after each six-month batch improve performance relative to a model that remains fixed and to persistence-based estimates? The answers define a local proof of concept for maintaining depth-resolved compaction information during delayed MLCW data delivery.
