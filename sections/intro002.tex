Excessive groundwater withdrawal is a principal cause of land subsidence in many aquifer-dependent regions worldwide \citep{galloway_land_1999,herrera-garcia_mapping_2021,jasechko_rapid_2024,huning_global_2024}. The resulting loss of land elevation can damage infrastructure, increase exposure to inundation, and reduce agricultural productivity \citep{tzampoglou_selected_2023}. An estimated 19\% of the global population lives in areas where the probability of subsidence is high or very high, and the exposed population is projected to increase further by 2040 \citep{herrera-garcia_mapping_2021}. Much of this exposure is concentrated in coastal plains and alluvial deltas \citep{nicholls_global_2021,buffardi_issue_2023}. Prominent cases associated with groundwater withdrawal include the Central Valley, Mexico City, and Jakarta, where cumulative displacement has reached several meters \citep{miller_rapid_2020,chaussard_over_2021,chaussard_sinking_2013}. The Mekong Delta presents a related regional hazard, with widespread subsidence occurring at rates of centimeters per year \citep{erban_groundwater_2014, minderhoud_2017_mekong}.

Effective mitigation depends on observations that reveal both ground surface displacement and deformation within the aquifer system. Spirit leveling provides accurate surface measurements but is commonly limited to annual or semiannual surveys because of the field work involved \citep{poland_guidebook_1984,zhu_leveling_2020}. Continuous Global Navigation Satellite System (cGNSS) stations record three-dimensional displacement at fixed locations, while Interferometric Synthetic Aperture Radar (InSAR) provides repeated measurements over broad areas \citep{bock_physical_2016,galloway_application_2007}. These methods differ in temporal and spatial coverage, but each describes deformation at the ground surface. They cannot determine how that deformation is distributed among subsurface depth intervals \citep{zhang_inverse_2016,burbey_extensometer_2020}. Observations of compaction at depth are therefore needed to connect surface displacement with deformation within the aquifer system.

Multilayer compaction monitoring wells (MLCWs) provide this information about deformation at depth. Magnetic rings anchored at different depths record relative displacement within a single borehole, allowing deformation to be separated among monitored aquifer and aquitard intervals \citep{hung2015_multiple,hung_measuring_2021}. The resulting records reveal where compaction is concentrated within the subsurface profile rather than only its combined expression at the ground surface. When interpreted with hydraulic head, these records can distinguish elastic and inelastic responses and support estimates of skeletal specific storage and safe groundwater levels \citep{hung_measuring_2021}. MLCWs therefore add direct evidence of deformation at depth that surface geodetic observations cannot provide.

Direct observations of deformation at depth are difficult to obtain across a dense monitoring network. Each MLCW requires a detailed investigation of subsurface stratigraphy before a deep well can be constructed and equipped with instruments at multiple depths. These requirements make each station costly to install and limit the number of locations that can be monitored. Continued operation also depends on repeated manual measurements \citep{hung_measuring_2021,hung2025_realtime}. Because the data provider processes and checks the measurements before release, the finalized record for a given month may not reach users until several months later. The monthly visits required to collect these measurements also add to the cost of operating the station. A schedule of one measurement every 6 or 12 months would reduce the number of visits and their associated costs, but it would also provide fewer MLCW observations for tracing how deformation at depth responds to hydraulic head changes. If measurements stopped altogether, no new MLCW observations would be available for comparing the estimates with measured deformation. Groundwater wells and cGNSS stations can continue to record hydraulic head and vertical displacement at monthly or shorter intervals. Hydraulic head changes shift effective stress and drive deformation within the aquifer system \citep{galloway_review_2011,chaussard_head_2014}, whereas cGNSS measures the total vertical displacement at the ground surface \citep{bock_physical_2016,yin_gps_2021}. Previous studies have shown that observations of surface displacement and hydraulic head can help determine how deformation is distributed with depth. \citet{jiang2018_insar} combined InSAR and hydraulic head to estimate elastic skeletal storativity and separate recoverable storage changes from irreversible loss, while \citet{smith2021_depth} used hydraulic head from three screened intervals with InSAR displacement to identify the interval responsible for most of the observed deformation. Process models informed by InSAR have also separated shallow and deeper contributions where several sources of subsidence overlap \citep{verberne2025_ravenna,verberne2025_dutch}. Direct observations at depth provided a second line of evidence, with \citet{hung2012_mlcw} combining MLCW, GPS, and hydraulic head in a one-dimensional compaction model and \citet{hung_measuring_2021} using MLCW records to locate major compaction intervals, estimate skeletal storage, and identify groundwater levels associated with recoverable deformation. Data-driven studies have addressed incomplete compaction records by reconstructing missing observations from hydrological, environmental, land use, sediment, and previously observed compaction information \citep{liu_reconstructing_2023,liu_deep_2025}. \citet{hung2025_realtime} instead used high-frequency extensometer records to forecast short-term displacement and compared the estimates with MLCW observations at corresponding depths. These studies show that hydraulic and surface observations can inform deformation at depth and that computational models can bridge gaps within established compaction records, but they do not establish how monthly estimates for MLCW-defined depth sections behave when finalized records are delayed, measurements are made less frequently, or later observations are unavailable.

The primary evaluation examined whether monthly observations of hydraulic head and cGNSS displacement could support estimates of deformation by depth while finalized MLCW records remained temporarily unavailable. It also examined how estimation error varied among depth sections and over the interval between successive data releases. The analysis then considered less frequent MLCW measurements after initial records of different lengths, focusing on monthly error, accumulated discrepancy, and uncertainty. A final evaluation treated the absence of subsequent MLCW observations as a limiting case and traced the monthly and accumulated discrepancies over time.
