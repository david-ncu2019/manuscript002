Excessive groundwater withdrawal is a principal cause of land subsidence in many aquifer-dependent regions worldwide \citep{galloway_land_1999,herrera-garcia_mapping_2021,jasechko_rapid_2024,huning_global_2024}. The resulting loss of land elevation can damage infrastructure, increase exposure to inundation, and reduce agricultural productivity \citep{tzampoglou_selected_2023}. An estimated 19\% of the global population lives in areas where the probability of subsidence is high or very high, and the exposed population is projected to increase further by 2040 \citep{herrera-garcia_mapping_2021}. Much of this exposure is concentrated in coastal plains and alluvial deltas \citep{nicholls_global_2021,buffardi_issue_2023}. Prominent cases associated with groundwater withdrawal include the Central Valley, Mexico City, and Jakarta, where cumulative displacement has reached several meters \citep{miller_rapid_2020,chaussard_over_2021,chaussard_sinking_2013}. The Mekong Delta presents a related regional hazard, with widespread subsidence occurring at rates of centimeters per year \citep{erban_groundwater_2014, minderhoud_2017_mekong}.

Effective mitigation depends on observations that reveal both ground surface displacement and deformation within the aquifer system. Spirit leveling provides accurate surface measurements but is commonly limited to annual or semiannual surveys because of the field work involved \citep{poland_guidebook_1984,zhu_leveling_2020}. Continuous Global Navigation Satellite System (cGNSS) stations record three-dimensional displacement at fixed locations, while Interferometric Synthetic Aperture Radar (InSAR) provides repeated measurements over broad areas \citep{bock_physical_2016,galloway_application_2007}. These methods differ in temporal and spatial coverage, but each describes deformation at the ground surface. They cannot determine how that deformation is distributed among subsurface depth intervals \citep{zhang_inverse_2016,burbey_extensometer_2020}. Observations of compaction at depth are therefore needed to connect surface displacement with deformation within the aquifer system.

Multilayer compaction monitoring wells (MLCWs) provide this information about deformation at depth. Magnetic rings anchored at different depths record relative displacement within a single borehole, allowing deformation to be separated among monitored aquifer and aquitard intervals \citep{hung2015_multiple,hung_measuring_2021}. The resulting records reveal where compaction is concentrated within the subsurface profile rather than only its combined expression at the ground surface. When interpreted with hydraulic head, these records can distinguish elastic and inelastic responses and support estimates of skeletal specific storage and safe groundwater levels \citep{hung_measuring_2021}. MLCWs therefore add direct evidence of deformation at depth that surface geodetic observations cannot provide.

Direct observations of deformation at depth are difficult to obtain across a dense monitoring network. Each MLCW requires a detailed investigation of subsurface stratigraphy before a deep well can be constructed and equipped with instruments at multiple depths. These requirements make each station costly to install and limit the number of locations that can be monitored. Continued operation also depends on repeated manual measurements \citep{hung_measuring_2021,hung2025_realtime}. Monthly measurements can still leave a temporary information gap because finalized records may reach users several months after the observations are made. The recurring field visits also contribute to the cost of maintaining the station. Extending the measurement interval from one month to 6 or 12 months could reduce this field work and its associated expense. The longer interval, however, would leave more time without a direct observation of deformation at depth. If MLCW measurements cease altogether, accumulated deformation can no longer be evaluated against new observations from the well. Where nearby groundwater wells and cGNSS stations are available, these systems can continue to record hydraulic head and vertical displacement at the ground surface at monthly or finer intervals. Hydraulic head reflects changes in pore water pressure and the corresponding effective stress within the aquifer system \citep{galloway_review_2011,chaussard_head_2014}, whereas cGNSS records the integrated vertical displacement at the ground surface \citep{bock_physical_2016,yin_gps_2021}. Neither source resolves deformation by depth. Previous studies have approached deformation at depth by combining surface displacement with hydraulic observations, physical models, or direct measurements within the aquifer system. \citet{jiang2018_insar} combined multitemporal InSAR and hydraulic head observations to estimate elastic skeletal storativity and separate recoverable groundwater storage changes from irreversible storage loss, finding that irreversible depletion dominated the total storage loss and lagged changes in hydraulic head. Using a related approach, \citet{smith2021_depth} integrated InSAR displacement with hydraulic head from three screened intervals to estimate storage properties, response times, and deformation within each interval. The method identified the interval responsible for most of the observed deformation, and the inferred pattern agreed with independent lithological evidence. Using a broader process framework, \citet{verberne2025_ravenna,verberne2025_dutch} used InSAR observations to refine models that separated subsidence associated with buildings, shallow sediments, drainage, and deeper extraction processes, showing that the largest surface displacement occurred where several contributions overlapped.

Direct measurements at depth provided another route to the same problem. \citet{hung2012_mlcw} combined MLCW, GPS, and hydraulic head observations with a one-dimensional compaction model to estimate hydrogeological properties and calculate deformation within individual aquifer and aquitard intervals. The calculated values agreed with observations, and the analysis showed why continued measurements were needed to keep the model consistent with changing aquifer conditions. \citet{hung_measuring_2021} subsequently used MLCW observations from numerous depths to identify where compaction was concentrated, distinguish reversible from irreversible deformation, and derive skeletal storage and safe groundwater levels. Other studies examined whether computational models could reconstruct incomplete compaction records or extend deformation estimates through time. \citet{liu_reconstructing_2023} used hydrological, environmental, land use, and sediment information to reconstruct missing MLCW records, while \citet{liu_deep_2025} combined monthly compaction, hydraulic head, rainfall, and electricity consumption associated with groundwater pumping to reconstruct missing observations and examine subsidence under reduced groundwater use. These analyses recovered important features of the observed records and linked deformation to groundwater conditions, but they addressed gaps within previously observed compaction series or scenarios of groundwater management rather than changes in the availability of new MLCW observations. \citet{hung2025_realtime} addressed forecasts over short periods by applying a model for time series to high-frequency extensometer observations and comparing the resulting estimates with MLCW observations at corresponding depths, thereby showing how continuous measurements at selected depths and MLCW measurements across many intervals provide different information. Previous work therefore provides clear precedents for combining hydraulic and deformation observations, assigning surface displacement to subsurface intervals, and extending incomplete deformation records. It remains uncertain, however, how well monthly hydraulic head and cGNSS observations can support estimates for depth sections defined by MLCW observations when finalized records are delayed, measurements are made less frequently, or subsequent observations are unavailable.
