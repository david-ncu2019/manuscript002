\subsection{Study Area Background}
\label{subsec:study_area}

The study area lies within the Choushui River Alluvial Fan (CRAF) on the western coastal plain of central Taiwan. The fan covers approximately 2,000~km$^2$ across Changhua and Yunlin counties. It is bounded by the Wu River to the north, the Beigang River to the south, the Bagua Tableland and Douliu Hills to the east, and the Taiwan Strait to the west. Surface elevation decreases from approximately 100--150~m near the eastern highlands to only several meters above mean sea level near the coast \citep{survey_project_1999, liu_characterization_2004}. Within this regional setting, the sediments beneath the CRAF were derived from weathering and erosion of slate, quartzite, shale, sandstone, and mudstone in the upstream watershed \citep{liu_characterization_2004, hung2015_multiple}. The fan is commonly divided into proximal, middle, and distal zones according to its hydrogeological characteristics \citep{survey_project_1999}. Gravel and coarse sand dominate the proximal fan and form highly permeable deposits. Grain size generally decreases westward, and fine sand, silt, and clay become increasingly interbedded across the middle and distal fans. This sedimentary transition forms a multilayer aquifer system in which permeable aquifers alternate with less permeable aquitards, as illustrated by the regional hydrogeological cross-section in \Cref{fig:studyarea}. These hydrogeological characteristics are important for understanding the effects of groundwater withdrawal across the CRAF, an agricultural region that produces approximately 35\% of Taiwan's rice together with maize, peanuts, wheat, onions, and other crops. Annual groundwater extraction has been estimated at 1.71--2.05 billion~m$^3$~yr$^{-1}$ \citep{tseng_estimating_2024}. Water demand increases during the first rice cultivation season from mid-February to late March, which overlaps the dry season from November to April \citep{chang_rice-field_2020}. Groundwater withdrawal is concentrated in the middle and distal fans. The resulting hydraulic head decline reduces pore-fluid pressure, transfers a greater share of the overburden load to the sediment skeleton, and increases effective stress. This stress increase compacts susceptible fine-grained aquitards and interbeds and contributes to land subsidence in these parts of the fan \citep{liu_characterization_2004, hung2015_multiple}.
