\subsection{Monthly estimation during delayed MLCW data delivery}
\label{subsec:results_delayed_delivery}

During each six-month estimation period, monthly hydraulic head and vertical surface displacement observations were used to estimate deformation increments for the six depth sections while the corresponding finalized MLCW records remained unavailable. Because observations from both sources were available through the corresponding month, the evaluation represented monthly estimation during delayed MLCW delivery rather than forecasting before those observations became available. The six-month interval defined the evaluation setting rather than a fixed data delivery schedule. This evaluation covered 23 complete cycles from 05/2013 to 10/2024 and yielded 138 estimates for each section. At the end of each cycle, the newly finalized monthly MLCW records were added to the calibration record before the next cycle (\Cref{fig:results_delayed_cycles}).

The monthly time series show that the estimates followed the observed variation more closely in S1--S4 than in S5--S6 (\Cref{fig:results_delayed_monthly_estimates}). This difference is also visible in the scatter plots, where estimates for S1--S4 remained closer to the line of exact agreement (\Cref{fig:results_delayed_scatter}).

This depth-dependent performance was also evident in the error statistics (\Cref{tab:delayed_performance_interval}). Across the six depth sections, RMSE ranged from 0.21 to 0.66~mm/month and MAE ranged from 0.15 to 0.50~mm/month. Errors were smaller in S1--S4 than in S5 and S6, with S5 showing the largest RMSE and MAE. The 90\% Bayesian posterior predictive intervals, however, showed a different depth pattern. They contained 65.9\% to 88.4\% of the observations and therefore did not attain nominal coverage in any section. Coverage was lowest in S6, whereas S5 reached 81.2\% despite having the largest RMSE and MAE. Possible sources of the coverage shortfall are considered in \Cref{subsec:discussion_limitations}.

Estimation performance was also evaluated by position within each six-month cycle to determine whether it changed as the interval without updated MLCW records lengthened. For each depth section, the first-month estimates from all 23 cycles were evaluated together, and the same procedure was repeated for months 2 through 6 (\Cref{tab:delayed_performance_by_month}). The resulting errors did not increase steadily as the cycle progressed. Average MAE ranged from 0.25 to 0.34~mm/month, and average RMSE ranged from 0.31 to 0.50~mm/month. Both metrics reached their highest values in month 2 rather than month 6, while average coverage varied from 73.2\% to 82.6\% without a consistent temporal pattern. The later months of the estimation period therefore did not show a progressive loss of estimation performance.

The fitted coefficients also varied among depth sections. To summarize these differences without reproducing all model coefficients, \Cref{tab:selected_coefficients} presents 14 variables whose 10th--90th percentile range remained entirely positive or negative in at least one section. Among these variables, surface displacement terms showed the clearest repeated directional patterns. The current surface displacement increment had a positive coefficient range in S1--S4 and S6, whereas hydraulic head terms showed fewer common patterns across sections. Seasonal coefficients also varied with depth. The dry-season indicator and annual sine component had opposite directions in S6 and the five shallower sections. These coefficients describe fitted associations and are interpreted separately in \Cref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{Estimation under reduced MLCW measurement frequency}
\label{subsec:results_reduced_frequency}

When only one cumulative MLCW observation was available at the end of each measurement interval, changing the interval from six to twelve months produced little change in monthly estimation accuracy at Tuku. Across the six combinations of measurement interval and initial-record length, average MAE ranged from 0.27 to 0.31~mm/month, average RMSE ranged from 0.37 to 0.41~mm/month, and average $R^2$ ranged from 0.66 to 0.70 (\Cref{tab:results_reduced_frequency}).

The monthly error series further showed that estimates based on 3, 5, and 8 years of initial observations generally changed in similar ways under both measurement schedules (\Cref{fig:results_reduced_frequency_6month,fig:results_reduced_frequency_12month}). Their frequent overlap indicated that increasing the initial record did not produce a persistent reduction in error. Consistent with this pattern, the 5-year record produced the lowest average MAE under both schedules, but the 8-year record did not improve upon either of the shorter records. At Tuku, extending the initial record beyond three years therefore did not consistently improve monthly estimation accuracy. The corresponding differences among depth sections are reported in \Cref{supp-tab:supp_reduced_frequency_monthly_full}.

The difference between the two measurement schedules became larger when the monthly estimates were accumulated until the next MLCW measurement. Average endpoint MAE across the six sections ranged from 1.405 to 1.542~mm for the 6-month schedule and from 2.470 to 2.738~mm for the 12-month schedule (\Cref{supp-tab:supp_reduced_frequency_endpoint_full}). This increase reflects the longer period over which monthly errors could accumulate before a new cumulative observation became available. Because the endpoints represented intervals of different lengths, these values do not establish that estimates during an equal portion of the two schedules differed in accuracy.

The Bayesian posterior predictive intervals provided a separate assessment of uncertainty in the monthly estimates. A nominal 90\% interval would be expected to contain approximately 90\% of the observed monthly increments, whereas the average coverage in these scenarios ranged from 77.8\% to 89.4\%. The intervals therefore represented less uncertainty than the observed errors required. Their average width increased with initial-record length under both schedules, but this increase did not consistently bring coverage closer to 90\%. Thus, the initial-record length had little systematic effect on monthly errors, while the longer measurement interval allowed a larger difference to accumulate before the next MLCW observation.

\subsection{Estimation without subsequent MLCW measurements}
\label{subsec:results_no_subsequent_mlcw}

After the initial calibration period, monthly hydraulic head and vertical surface displacement observations continued to provide model inputs, while no subsequent MLCW measurements were incorporated. For the 3-year initial record, the correspondence between observed and estimated monthly deformation increments was clearest in S1--S4 (\Cref{fig:results_no_subsequent_mlcw_timeseries}). The corresponding time series for the 5- and 8-year initial records are provided in \Cref{supp-fig:supp_no_subsequent_mlcw_timeseries}.

This correspondence was also reflected in the monthly error statistics. Average $R^2$ across the six depth sections ranged from 0.67 to 0.71, average MAE ranged from 0.26 to 0.29~mm/month, and average RMSE ranged from 0.36 to 0.40~mm/month (\Cref{tab:results_no_subsequent_mlcw_interval}). The running MAE and RMSE varied during the evaluation period without a common increase across the six depth sections (\Cref{supp-fig:supp_no_subsequent_mlcw_running_errors}). Extending the initial record from three to eight years therefore did not produce a consistent reduction in monthly estimation error.

Although monthly errors remained similar, their cumulative effects developed differently among the depth sections and initial-record lengths. By month 80, the average absolute cumulative error across the six sections was 7.393, 6.686, and 4.286~mm for the 3-, 5-, and 8-year initial records, respectively (\Cref{fig:results_no_subsequent_mlcw_cumulative_error,tab:results_no_subsequent_mlcw_h80}). S4 had the largest final error under all three initial-record lengths. The 8-year record reduced this error from 15.075 to 8.747~mm, although it did not produce the smallest final error in every section.

The posterior predictive intervals provided a separate description of the monthly estimates. Average coverage of the nominal 90\% intervals was 65.6, 79.8, and 78.8\% for the 3-, 5-, and 8-year initial records, respectively, while average interval width increased from 0.62 to 1.23~mm (\Cref{tab:results_no_subsequent_mlcw_interval}). The three initial records therefore produced similar monthly errors but different cumulative-error trajectories and posterior interval statistics over the common evaluation period.
